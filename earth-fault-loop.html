    Why it matters

    A breaker clears an earth fault quickly only when the fault current is big enough to trip it magnetically. A Type B breaker needs 5 × its rating to be sure of that: 160 A for a B32.

    Fault current is U₀Zs. Double the loop impedance and you halve the current. That's why long runs of thin cable can fail, even though nothing is wrong with them.

    On a TT system the loop goes through the ground, so Zs is tens or hundreds of ohms. The fault current is a few amps, far too small to trip a breaker. An RCD does the job instead: it trips on as little as 30 mA of imbalance.

    On site, you measure Ze at the origin with the main earthing conductor disconnected, and measure R1+R2 during dead testing. You can then check a live Zs reading against Ze + (R1+R2).

    Learning prototype. Not for design or certification. Check every figure against the current BS 7671, the On-Site Guide and Guidance Note 3, and with your tutor.

    Maximum Zs values follow BS 7671 Table 41.3 (0.4 s, U₀ = 230 V, Cmin = 0.95). The 80% figure is the usual rule of thumb for readings taken at room temperature. The design check multiplies R1+R2 by 1.20 for 70 °C thermoplastic cable. Conductor resistances (mΩ/m at 20 °C) are from the On-Site Guide. Radial circuits only; ring finals work differently.
