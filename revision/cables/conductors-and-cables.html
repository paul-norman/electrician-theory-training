Cables

Conductors and cables

What a cable is made of, why it's almost always copper, how cable sizes are measured, and why bigger loads need bigger cables.

Key takeaways

A cable is made up of conductors, insulation and an outer sheath. The best conductors are silver, then copper, gold and aluminium; copper is used for almost all wiring because it conducts nearly as well as silver at a fraction of the price. A conductor's resistance is R = ρ × LA: it rises with length and falls with cross-sectional area. Cable sizes are that area (CSA) in mm², not a diameter: "2.5 mm²" cable has conductors about 1.8 mm across. Twin and earth has brown and blue insulated cores and a bare copper CPC, which must be sleeved green and yellow wherever it's exposed. Current heats a conductor (P = I²R); general-purpose PVC insulation is rated for conductors up to 70 °C. So a bigger load needs a bigger CSA, and how much current a cable can safely carry also depends on how easily its heat can escape.

What's in a cable

Every cable has the same three layers:

  • Conductors carry the current. Usually copper.
  • Insulation around each conductor stops current leaking between them or to anything else. It's colour coded so you can tell the conductors apart.
  • The sheath wraps everything and protects the insulation from knocks, abrasion and dirt.

The everyday cable for house wiring is flat twin and earth (often just "T&E"). It has a brown line core, a blue neutral core and, between them, a bare copper conductor used as the circuit protective conductor (CPC). Because that CPC has no insulation inside the cable, you must slide green-and-yellow sleeving over it wherever it's exposed, at every accessory and junction.

Cable sizes are areas, not diameters

Cables are sized by the cross-sectional area (CSA) of each conductor, in square millimetres: the area of the copper face you'd see if you cut straight through it. "6 mm² cable" doesn't mean 6 mm across; a solid 6 mm² conductor is only about 2.8 mm across.

To get the diameter from the area: d = 2 × √Aπ. Doubling the area only makes the conductor about 1.4 times wider.

Common sizes are 1.0, 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10 and 16 mm². BS 7671 sets 1.0 mm² copper as the smallest for the fixed wiring of power and lighting circuits.

Choosing the conductor metal

A material's resistivity (ρ, the Greek letter rho) says how strongly it resists current, regardless of the conductor's shape. Lower is better.

Silver1.59 × 10−8 Ω·mAbout 6% betterSwitch and relay contacts. Too expensive for cable.
Copper1.72 × 10−8 Ω·m—Almost all wiring: conducts nearly as well as silver, flexible, easy to terminate, affordable.
Gold2.44 × 10−8 Ω·mAbout 40% worseA thin plating on connector contacts. It doesn't corrode, so the contact surface stays clean.
Aluminium2.82 × 10−8 Ω·mAbout 64% worseOverhead lines and large supply cables, where its lightness and low cost matter. Needs a bigger CSA for the same resistance.

Gold is used on contacts not because it conducts best, but because it doesn't corrode. Copper and silver gradually form surface layers (oxide on copper, tarnish on silver) that add resistance where two surfaces touch.

Resistance of a conductor

Resistance goes up with length and down with area. With the resistivity of the metal, that gives:

R = ρ × LA

Here L is the length in metres and A the area in m² (1 mm² = 10−6 m²). Double the length and the resistance doubles; double the area and it halves.

Insulation and heat

Current through a conductor's resistance makes heat (P = I² × R, see Power). The heat has to escape through the insulation and sheath into the surroundings. If it can't escape fast enough, the conductor gets hotter and hotter.

The insulation sets the limit. General-purpose PVC (a thermoplastic, meaning it softens when heated) is rated for a conductor temperature of up to 70 °C. Above that it starts to soften and age quickly, and the cable can eventually fail or catch fire. Cables with thermosetting insulation, such as XLPE, can run at up to 90 °C.

Why bigger loads need bigger cables

A bigger load draws more current (I = PV, and V is fixed at 230 V). More current means more heat in the cable, and because heat grows with I², it grows fast. The answer is a bigger CSA: lower resistance means less heat for the same current, and a larger cable also has more surface to lose heat from.

How much current a cable can carry safely isn't fixed by its size alone. It also depends on how easily the heat gets away: a cable clipped to a wall in open air can carry more than the same cable buried in loft insulation or bundled with others. Thicker insulation doesn't help; insulation traps heat. Long runs also matter, because more resistance means more voltage lost along the way. Working all this out is cable sizing, which comes later in the course.
