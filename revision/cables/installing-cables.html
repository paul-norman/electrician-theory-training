Installing cables

Which cable to use, how to identify its conductors, and where it can safely go: in walls, through joists and under the garden.

Key takeaways

Flat twin and earth (6242Y) is the everyday cable in homes; the low smoke, zero halogen version is 6242B, and three-core and earth is used for two-way switching. Look for marks of conformity such as BASEC on cable. The harmonised colours (brown line, blue neutral; brown, black and grey for three-phase) replaced red and black from 2004, and where old meets new both are marked and a caution notice is fitted. Cables buried in walls less than 50 mm deep must run in the safe zones (within 150 mm of the ceiling or a corner, or in line with an accessory), or be mechanically protected or RCD-protected. Vertical chases can be up to a third of the wall's thickness, horizontal ones a sixth. Holes in joists go on the centre line, between 0.25 and 0.4 of the span from the support, no bigger than a quarter of the joist's depth. Cables buried in the garden go at least 500 mm deep (600 mm where the ground may be double dug), with marker tape about 150 mm below the surface.

Marks of conformity show a product has been made and tested to a standard: BASEC for cables, the BSI Kitemark, ASTA BEAB and ENEC for electrical equipment, and the CE (or UKCA) mark, which is the manufacturer's own declaration. Cheap, unmarked cable can have undersized conductors or poor insulation.

Colours, old and new

UK wiring colours were harmonised with Europe. The new colours could be used from 31 March 2004 and have been required from 1 April 2006:

ConductorOld colourCurrent colour
Single-phase lineRedBrown
NeutralBlackBlue
Three-phase L1, L2, L3Red, yellow, blueBrown, black, grey
Protective conductorGreen and yellow (plain green in very old installations)Green and yellow

The trap is black: in old wiring it's the neutral; in new three-phase wiring it's a line (L2). And old blue was a line, but new blue is the neutral. So where new wiring is connected to old, both sides are marked at the interface (L and N, or L1, L2, L3 and N), and a caution notice at the distribution board warns that the installation has wiring colours to two versions of BS 7671.

Conductors used for something other than their colour suggests must be re-identified. A blue conductor used as a switched line (the switch wire to a light) gets a brown sleeve or tape at each end. The bare CPC in twin and earth is sleeved green and yellow wherever it's exposed.

Cables in walls: the safe zones

A cable buried in a wall or partition at less than 50 mm from the surface could be hit by a nail, screw or drill. BS 7671 (regulation 522.6.202) says it must either run in a safe zone, where people expect cables to be, or be protected:

  • Safe zones: within 150 mm of the top of the wall (the ceiling) or of a corner where two walls meet, and horizontally or vertically in line with an accessory, such as a socket or switch.
  • Or protection: earthed metal conduit, trunking or covering, mechanical protection strong enough to stop a nail, or additional protection by a 30 mA RCD. In homes, the RCD is the usual answer, and it's needed anyway for most circuits.

Cables in a partition with metal studs or framing need RCD or earthed-metal protection at any depth, because a nail through the cable could make the frame live.

Chases, joists and notches

Cutting into the building can weaken it, so Approved Document A sets limits (they're summarised in the Electrician's Guide to the Building Regulations):

  • Chases in walls: vertical chases no deeper than a third of the thickness of the wall (or leaf); horizontal chases no deeper than a sixth.
  • Holes in joists: on the centre line (the neutral axis, where the joist is under least stress), no larger than 0.25 × the joist's depth in diameter, and between 0.25 and 0.4 × the span from the support. Cables through joists must also be at least 50 mm from the top and bottom edges, out of reach of floorboard nails, or be protected.
  • Notches: in the top edge only, no deeper than 0.125 × the joist's depth, and between 0.07 and 0.25 × the span from the support.

Cables in the ground

A cable to a garden building or outdoor light usually runs underground in SWA cable, or in a cable with similar earthed protection, or in ducting designed for it. Guidance in the Electrician's Guide and the On-Site Guide:

  • bury it at least 500 mm deep, or 600 mm where the ground may be double dug, such as a vegetable patch
  • lay marker tape or cable tiles along the route, about 150 mm below the surface, so anyone digging finds the warning before the cable
  • record the route on the installation's drawings

BS 7671 itself asks for a depth that avoids damage from reasonably foreseeable disturbance, with the cable identified by covers or marking tape (regulation 522.8.10).

Supports, heat and fire

  • Premature collapse: wiring must be supported so it can't fall in a fire and trap firefighters. Plastic clips and trunking alone aren't enough where they'd be the only support; use metal clips, ties or supports.
  • Heat: keep cables away from hot pipes and flues, and remember that thermal insulation reduces a cable's rating (see Cable sizing and voltage drop).
  • Fire barriers: where a cable passes through a wall or floor that resists fire, seal the hole to restore it.
