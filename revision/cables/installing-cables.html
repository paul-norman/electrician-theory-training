Installing cables

Which cable to use, how to identify its conductors, and where it can safely go: in walls, through joists and under the garden.

Key takeaways

Flat twin and earth (6242Y) is the everyday cable in homes; the low smoke, zero halogen version is 6242B, and three-core and earth is used for two-way switching. Look for marks of conformity such as BASEC on cable. The harmonised colours (brown line, blue neutral; brown, black and grey for three-phase) replaced red and black from 2004, and where old meets new both are marked and a caution notice is fitted. Cables buried in walls less than 50 mm deep must run in the safe zones (within 150 mm of the ceiling or a corner, or in line with an accessory), or be mechanically protected or RCD-protected. Vertical chases can be up to a third of the wall's thickness, horizontal ones a sixth. Holes in joists go on the centre line, between 0.25 and 0.4 of the span from the support, no bigger than a quarter of the joist's depth. Cables buried in the garden go at least 500 mm deep (600 mm where the ground may be double dug), with marker tape about 150 mm below the surface.

Choosing the cable

Marks of conformity show a product has been made and tested to a standard: BASEC for cables, the BSI Kitemark, ASTA BEAB and ENEC for electrical equipment, and the CE (or UKCA) mark, which is the manufacturer's own declaration. Cheap, unmarked cable can have undersized conductors or poor insulation.

Colours, old and new

UK wiring colours were harmonised with Europe. The new colours could be used from 31 March 2004 and have been required from 1 April 2006:

Functional earthCreamPink

The trap is black: in old wiring it's the neutral; in new three-phase wiring it's a line (L2). And old blue was a line, but new blue is the neutral. So where new wiring is connected to old, both sides are marked at the interface (L and N, or L1, L2, L3 and N). Older installations also had a caution notice at the board warning of "wiring colours to two versions of BS 7671". That notice stopped being required in 2022 (Amendment 2), but you can still fit one, and you'll see plenty of them.

Old colours on their own aren't a reason to rewire: millions of homes still have red and black wiring that tests fine. Look at its condition instead. A sticky green goo oozing from accessories is a sign of old PVC cable breaking down (its plasticiser reacting with the copper), and suggests the wiring needs replacing. Don't handle it with bare hands.

Conductors used for something other than their colour suggests must be re-identified. A blue conductor used as a switched line (the switch wire to a light) gets a brown sleeve or tape at each end. The bare CPC in twin and earth is sleeved green and yellow wherever it's exposed.

Cables in walls: the safe zones

Nobody can see a cable once it's buried in a wall, so BS 7671 controls where it goes and how it's protected against nails and screws (regulation 522.6, "impact"). The safe zones (the regulations call them prescribed zones) are where people should expect cables to be:

  • horizontally or vertically in line with an accessory, such as a socket or switch, so you can run straight up, down or sideways to it
  • within 150 mm of the top of the wall (the ceiling), and within 150 mm of a corner where two walls meet. Use these bands to get to an accessory you can't reach in a straight line, then turn off to it.

Most people know not to drill above or beside a socket, but very few know about the bands along the ceiling and down the corners.

Amendment 4 put the rules for masonry walls and for partitions into one table in regulation 522.6.202 (they used to be two regulations, 522.6.202 and 522.6.203). Ask two questions: is the cable less than 50 mm from the surface, and does the wall have metal parts, such as metal studs? Check the exact wording in your copy, but in outline:

WallCable less than 50 mm deepCable 50 mm or more deep
No metal parts (brick, block, timber studs)In a safe zone and 30 mA RCD protection, or protected to 522.6.204No extra requirement
Metal parts (such as metal studs)30 mA RCD protection, or in a safe zone and protected to 522.6.20430 mA RCD protection, or protected to 522.6.204

Metal studs matter because their sharp edges can cut a cable, and a nail through the cable could make the whole frame live. Regulation 522.6.204 lists the alternatives to an RCD:

  • an earthed metal covering on the cable, such as the armour of SWA
  • earthed metal conduit or trunking
  • mechanical protection strong enough to stop nails and screws, such as steel plates
  • a SELV or PELV circuit

In homes, the 30 mA RCD is the usual answer, because almost every circuit needs one anyway. It's an example of additional protection: if someone does put a nail through a cable, the RCD disconnects quickly enough to limit the shock. Whenever you see "additional protection", think 30 mA RCD (an RCBO counts, as it has one built in).

These rules are for you when you're installing the cable and can see the wall construction. Keep your books in the van and check them on site. That's what they're for.

Chases

Cutting into the building can weaken it, so Approved Document A sets limits (they're summarised in the Electrician's Guide to the Building Regulations):

A chase is a groove cut into a wall to bury a cable. Vertical chases must be no deeper than a third of the thickness of the wall (or leaf); horizontal chases no deeper than a sixth. A brick leaf is about 100 mm thick, so that's about 33 mm vertically and 16 mm horizontally, measured into the masonry, not including the plaster. Horizontal chases are limited more because a groove running along a wall weakens it far more than one running up it. Keep chases to the safe zones (see above), and fill them back flush.

Joists and notches

Floor and ceiling joists carry the building's weight, so holes and notches are limited to the places where they take least strength away (also from Approved Document A):

  • Holes in joists: on the centre line (the neutral axis, where the joist is under least stress), no larger than 0.25 × the joist's depth in diameter, and between 0.25 and 0.4 × the span from the support. Cables through joists must also be at least 50 mm from the top and bottom edges, out of reach of floorboard nails and ceiling fixings (regulation 522.6.201). If that isn't possible, run the cable in earthed steel conduit or fit steel plates over it.
  • Notches: in the top edge only, no deeper than 0.125 × the joist's depth, and between 0.07 and 0.25 × the span from the support.

Drilling is far quicker: with a sharp flat wood bit, each hole takes seconds, while a notch takes several minutes. Plumbers notch more often, because rigid pipes are hard to feed through holes.

Cables in the ground

A cable to a garden building or outdoor light usually runs underground in SWA cable, or in a cable with similar earthed protection, or in ducting designed for it. Guidance in the Electrician's Guide and the On-Site Guide:

  • bury it at least 500 mm deep, or 600 mm where the ground may be double dug, such as a vegetable patch
  • lay marker tape or cable tiles along the route, about 150 mm below the surface, so anyone digging finds the warning before the cable
  • record the route on the installation's drawings

The armour is earthed, which is the point. A spade that hits the cable touches the earthed armour first. The spade then can't reach a dangerous voltage, and if it does reach a live conductor, the fault goes straight to earth through the armour, so the protective device operates.

BS 7671 itself asks for a depth that avoids damage from reasonably foreseeable disturbance, with the cable identified by covers or marking tape (regulation 522.8.10).

Supports, heat and fire

  • Premature collapse: wiring must be supported so it can't fall in a fire and trap firefighters. Plastic clips and trunking alone aren't enough where they'd be the only support; use metal clips, ties or supports. The rule (Regulation 521.10.202) followed firefighter deaths, including two at Shirley Towers in Southampton in 2010, where cables fell when their plastic fixings melted and the firefighters became tangled in them. There's no set spacing for metal fixings: use enough that the run can't drop.
  • Heat: keep cables away from hot pipes and flues, and remember that thermal insulation reduces a cable's rating (see Cable sizing and voltage drop).
  • Fire barriers: where a cable passes through a wall or floor that resists fire, seal the hole to restore it.
