Lighting circuits

How a lighting circuit is wired from ceiling rose to ceiling rose, and how one light can be switched from one, two or many places.

Key takeaways

A domestic lighting circuit is a radial, usually in 1.0 or 1.5 mm² twin and earth on a 6 A Type B device, with 30 mA RCD protection. It's wired either by the loop-in method, from ceiling rose to ceiling rose, or with junction boxes. The switch always goes in the line: the cable down to a switch carries the permanent line down and the switched line back, and the blue conductor doing that job is sleeved brown. One-way switching controls a light from one place. Two-way switching uses two two-way switches joined by two strappers (three-core and earth cable), so either switch changes the light. Add intermediate switches between them for three or more places. Extra-low voltage lighting is fed through a safety isolating transformer or driver.

The circuit

Lighting circuits are radials: one cable runs out from the consumer unit and on from light to light. A typical domestic circuit uses 1.0 or 1.5 mm² twin and earth, protected by a 6 A Type B circuit breaker or RCBO (see Consumer units and protective devices). In homes, BS 7671 requires 30 mA RCD protection for lighting circuits (regulation 411.3.4). Splitting the house over two or more lighting circuits means one fault never leaves the whole house in darkness.

Loop-in and junction box wiring

  • Loop-in is the usual method in homes. The circuit cable goes into each ceiling rose and out again to the next, and the switch cable drops from the rose to the switch. All the connections are at the rose, so they can be reached from below.
  • Junction box wiring puts the connections in a box in the ceiling void, with separate cables to the light and the switch. It's used where a fitting can't take several cables, such as many modern light fittings and downlights. The junction box must stay accessible, unless it's a maintenance-free type.

A loop-in ceiling rose has four sets of terminals:

Terminal blockWhat connects there
Loop (permanent line)Line in, line out to the next rose, and the line going down to the switch. Always live, even with the light off.
NeutralNeutral in, neutral out, and the neutral to the lamp flex.
Switch lineThe switched line coming back from the switch (blue conductor sleeved brown), and the brown of the lamp flex.
EarthAll the CPCs, sleeved green and yellow, and the earth of the flex for a Class I fitting.

Because the loop terminal is always live, turning a light off at the switch doesn't make the rose safe to work on. Isolate the circuit (see Safe isolation).

One-way, two-way and intermediate

  • One-way: a single switch with two terminals, controlling a light from one place. Twin and earth runs to the switch.
  • Two-way: for a light switched from two places, such as the top and bottom of the stairs. Each two-way switch has a common terminal (COM) and two others (L1 and L2). The two switches are joined by two strappers in three-core and earth cable. The light is on when both switches connect their commons to the same strapper; operating either switch changes that.
  • Intermediate: for three or more places, such as a long landing. Intermediate switches go in the strappers between the two two-way switches. Each one either passes the strappers straight through or crosses them over, so every switch changes the light.

In three-core and earth, the cores are brown, black and grey. Where they're used as strappers or switched lines, they're identified as line conductors (with brown sleeving where needed), because all of them can be live.

Fittings and extra-low voltage lighting

  • Class I light fittings and metal switch plates must be connected to the CPC. Even Class II fittings should have a CPC available at the point, in case a Class I fitting is fitted later.
  • For Edison screw lampholders, the centre contact goes to the line, so the screw shell that can be touched is on the neutral.
  • Extra-low voltage lighting, such as 12 V downlights and LED strip, is fed through a safety isolating transformer or LED driver: 230 V in, extra-low voltage out. Keep the transformer or driver ventilated and accessible, and remember the cables on the low-voltage side carry a much bigger current for the same power.
