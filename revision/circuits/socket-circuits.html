Socket circuits: rings and radials

The UK's ring final circuit, the radial circuits that are replacing it in many new homes, and the rules for adding spurs.

Key takeaways

A ring final circuit leaves the consumer unit, loops through every socket and returns to the same device, so each socket is fed from both directions. The standard ring is 2.5/1.5 mm² twin and earth on a 30 or 32 A device, serving a floor area of up to 100 m². Radials run out and stop: 2.5 mm² on 20 A for up to 50 m², or 4 mm² on 30 or 32 A for up to 75 m². A non-fused spur from a ring may feed only one single or double socket (or one fixed appliance), in cable at least as big as the ring, and the number of non-fused spurs mustn't exceed the number of sockets and fixed appliances on the ring itself. A fused spur starts at a fused connection unit (13 A maximum), so its cable only needs to suit the fuse. Sockets up to 32 A need 30 mA RCD protection.

The ring final circuit

The ring is a British design from the 1940s. Both ends of the cable connect to the same 30 or 32 A device, so current reaches each socket along two parallel paths and the load is shared between the two legs. That lets 2.5 mm² cable, rated well below 32 A on its own, serve a 32 A circuit: BS 7671 Appendix 15 requires the cable's current-carrying capacity to be at least 0.67 × the device rating.

The catch is that a ring needs to stay a ring. A break in one leg leaves every socket still working, fed from one side only, and nobody notices that the remaining cable could now be overloaded. That's what the ring final continuity test finds (see Dead tests).

Standard circuits

BS 7671 Appendix 15 and the On-Site Guide (Appendix H) give standard socket circuits that can be used without further calculation, provided the conditions are met (cable lengths, installation method, Zs):

There's no limit on the number of sockets within that floor area, but kitchens and utility rooms, with their heavy appliances, often get a circuit of their own. Many new homes now use radials throughout: they're simpler to test and fault-find, and a broken conductor shows up straight away.

Spurs

  • Non-fused spur: a branch from a ring, taken from a socket's terminals or a junction box on the ring. It may feed one single or one double socket, or one fixed appliance, and its cable must be no smaller than the ring's. The number of non-fused spurs must not exceed the number of sockets and fixed appliances connected directly on the ring.
  • Fused spur: a branch taken through a fused connection unit (FCU), with a fuse of no more than 13 A. Because the fuse protects the spur, its cable only has to suit the fuse, not the 32 A device. A fused spur often feeds a fixed appliance such as a boiler, extractor fan or towel rail.
  • Never take a spur from a spur: the extra load would all come through the first spur's cable. (A radial is different: it can branch freely, because every part of it is already sized for the device.)

Placing sockets

  • Height: in new homes, between 450 and 1200 mm from the floor (Approved Document M; see Laws and guidance).
  • Kitchens: guidance is to keep sockets at least 300 mm horizontally from the edge of a sink, and never above a gas or electric hob. Appliances hidden behind units are fed from sockets or outlets controlled by accessible switches, such as a switched FCU or a grid switch above the worktop.
  • Bathrooms: at least 3 m from zone 1 (see Bathrooms and special locations).
  • Outdoors: a weatherproof socket with a suitable IP rating, with 30 mA RCD protection.

Every socket-outlet rated up to 32 A needs additional protection by a 30 mA RCD (regulation 411.3.3), and in homes there's no exception.
