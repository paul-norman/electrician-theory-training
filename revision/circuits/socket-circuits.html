Socket circuits: rings and radials

The UK's ring final circuit, the radial circuits that are replacing it in many new homes, and the rules for adding spurs.

Key takeaways

A ring final circuit leaves the consumer unit, loops through every socket and returns to the same device, so each socket is fed from both directions. It isn't a short circuit: both ends of the line go into the same breaker terminal, both neutrals into the neutral bar and both CPCs into the earth bar, so line only ever meets line. The standard ring is 2.5/1.5 mm² twin and earth on a 30 or 32 A device, serving a floor area of up to 100 m². Radials run out and stop: 2.5 mm² on 20 A for up to 50 m², or 4 mm² on 30 or 32 A for up to 75 m². A non-fused spur from a ring may feed only one single or double socket (or one fixed appliance), in cable at least as big as the ring, and the number of non-fused spurs mustn't exceed the number of sockets and fixed appliances on the ring itself. A fused spur starts at a fused connection unit (13 A maximum), so its cable only needs to suit the fuse. Sockets up to 32 A need 30 mA RCD protection.

How can both ends go back to the board?

It looks wrong at first: a cable that leaves the board and comes straight back sounds like a short circuit. The trick is to stop thinking about the cable and think about each conductor on its own.

  • Both ends of the brown (line) go into the same terminal of the circuit breaker. So the brown is one closed loop, and every point on it is at 230 V.
  • Both ends of the blue (neutral) go into the neutral bar: another closed loop, at 0 V.
  • Both ends of the CPC go into the earth bar.

Line only ever meets line, and neutral meets neutral, so nothing is shorted. A short circuit is line touching neutral. Each socket simply bridges the brown loop to the blue loop. When something is plugged in, current can reach it from the breaker by going either way round the brown loop, and it returns either way round the blue. It's like a ring main of water pipe fed from one tap: the water can reach any outlet from both directions.

Because there are two routes, the current shares itself between them: more goes the short way, less the long way (the shorter path has less resistance; see Series and parallel circuits). With the load in the middle, each leg carries half. That sharing is the whole point of a ring, and it's why the cable can be thinner than the breaker rating suggests.

The ring final circuit

The ring is a British design from the 1940s. Both ends of the cable connect to the same 30 or 32 A device, so current reaches each socket along two parallel paths and the load is shared between the two legs. That lets 2.5 mm² cable, rated well below 32 A on its own, serve a 32 A circuit: regulation 433.1.204 accepts it as long as the cable's current-carrying capacity (Iz, after any correction factors) is at least 20 A, and the load is spread so no part of the ring carries more than that for long periods.

It was designed in the 1940s, when copper was short after the war: a ring covers a big floor area with less cable than radials would. Many electricians now prefer radials, but you'll meet rings in most existing homes, usually one downstairs, one upstairs and sometimes one just for the kitchen.

To keep the load spread, cookers, ovens and hobs over 2 kW get their own radial circuit, not the ring (Appendix 15). Rings are for socket-outlets and fused connection units only: never lighting or a cooker.

The catch is that a ring needs to stay a ring. A break in one leg leaves every socket still working, fed from one side only, and nobody notices that the remaining cable could now be overloaded. That's what the ring final continuity test finds (see Dead tests).

Designing a ring final

A ring follows the same design steps as any circuit (see Cable sizing and voltage drop), with three twists:

  • Design current: there's no single load, because nobody knows what will be plugged in. The On-Site Guide (Table 7.1) gives an assumed load to use: 26 A for a 32 A ring.
  • Cable rating: divide 20 A by the correction factors, not the 32 A device rating, because each leg only has to carry part of the load (regulation 433.1.204).
  • Voltage drop: divide by 4,000, not 1,000, using the total length of the ring. Assume the load is in the middle: the current splits into two halves, and each travels only half the ring, so the drop is a quarter of a radial's.

Spurs

  • Non-fused spur: a branch from a ring, taken from a socket's terminals or a junction box on the ring. It may feed one single or one double socket, or one fixed appliance, and its cable must be no smaller than the ring's. The number of non-fused spurs must not exceed the number of sockets and fixed appliances connected directly on the ring.
  • Fused spur: a branch taken through a fused connection unit (FCU), with a fuse of no more than 13 A. Because the fuse protects the spur, its cable only has to suit the fuse, not the 32 A device. A fused spur often feeds a fixed appliance such as a boiler, extractor fan or towel rail.
  • Selectivity: fusing down also means a fault on the spur blows the small fuse in the FCU, not the circuit's 32 A device, so the rest of the ring stays on. Selectivity means the device nearest the fault operates first. A 100 W security light (0.43 A) fed from a ring through an FCU with a 3 A fuse can use 0.75 mm² flex.
  • Never take a spur from a spur: the extra load would all come through the first spur's cable. (A radial is different: it can branch freely, because every part of it is already sized for the device.)

Placing sockets

  • Height: in new homes, between 450 and 1200 mm from the floor (Approved Document M; see Laws and guidance). In an existing home you can match the existing heights, as long as the work doesn't make the building less compliant than it was and the sockets are still usable: one so low that a plug strains its flex isn't.
  • Kitchens: guidance is to keep sockets at least 300 mm horizontally from the edge of a sink, about 150 mm above the worktop, and never above a gas or electric hob. The Electrician's Guide to the Building Regulations has a diagram. Appliances hidden behind units are fed from sockets or outlets controlled by accessible switches, such as a switched FCU or a grid switch above the worktop.
  • Bathrooms: at least 2.5 m from zone 1 (see Bathrooms and special locations).
  • Outdoors: a weatherproof socket with a suitable IP rating, with 30 mA RCD protection.

Every socket-outlet rated up to 32 A needs additional protection by a 30 mA RCD (regulation 411.3.3), and in homes there's no exception.
