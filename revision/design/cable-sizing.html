Cable sizing and voltage drop

A cable's current rating depends on how easily it can get rid of heat. Bury it in insulation or bunch it with others and it can carry less. Then it also has to deliver enough voltage at the far end.

Key takeaways

Find the installation reference method (BS 7671 Table 4A2), such as C for clipped direct or 100 to 103 for twin and earth in or near thermal insulation. Then apply correction factors: Ca for ambient temperature (Table 4B1, 1.0 at 30 °C), Cg for grouping (Table 4C1) and Ci for thermal insulation (0.5 if surrounded for more than 0.5 m). The tabulated current the cable needs is It ≥ InCa × Cg × Ci. Pick the smallest size in the current-rating table for that method that meets it. Then check voltage drop = mV/A/m × Ib × L1,000 against the limits for a public supply: 3% (6.9 V) for lighting, 5% (11.5 V) for everything else. If it's too high, go up a size.

Heat is the limit

Current through a cable's resistance makes heat (P = I² × R; see Power). The cable settles at the temperature where it loses heat as fast as it makes it. Its current-carrying capacity is the current that keeps it at or below its maximum working temperature: 70 °C for ordinary PVC (thermoplastic) twin and earth (see Conductors and cables). Anything that stops heat escaping lowers that current.

Correction factors

The tables assume standard conditions. When the real conditions are worse, multiply by a factor less than 1:

  • Ca, ambient temperature (Table 4B1). The tables assume 30 °C air. In a hot plant room at 35 °C, Ca is 0.94 for PVC cable; at 25 °C it's 1.03, a small bonus.
  • Cg, grouping (Table 4C1). Cables bunched together warm each other. Three circuits bunched or enclosed together: 0.70. Six: 0.57.
  • Ci, thermal insulation. A cable totally surrounded by insulation for more than 0.5 m: 0.5, which halves its rating. Shorter lengths have smaller reductions (Table 52.2). Don't apply Ci on top of methods 100 to 103, which already allow for the insulation.
  • Cc: 0.725 where the protective device is a rewirable fuse (see Design current and diversity), and 0.9 for cables buried in the ground.

Divide the device rating by all the factors that apply to get the rating to look up in the table:

It ≥ InCa × Cg × Ci × Cc

Voltage drop

Long, thin cables lose voltage along their length, and equipment at the far end gets less than it needs. BS 7671 Appendix 4 gives a voltage drop for each cable size in millivolts per amp per metre (mV/A/m). It already counts both the line and neutral, so use the cable's route length:

Vdrop = mV/A/m × Ib × L1,000

Use the design current Ib here, not the device rating. For an installation fed from the public supply, the limits from the origin to the load are 3% for lighting (6.9 V at 230 V) and 5% for other uses (11.5 V). For a final circuit fed from a sub-board, add the sub-main's drop to the final circuit's.

Two more checks to finish a design

A cable that passes on current and voltage drop still has to pass on fault protection: Zs = Ze + (R1 + R2) must be low enough for the device to disconnect in time (see Automatic disconnection). And the CPC must be big enough to carry the fault current until then without overheating, which BS 7671 checks with the adiabatic equation, S = √(I² × t)k, in regulation 543.1.3. Twin and earth with the standard CPC usually passes in ordinary domestic circuits, but it has to be checked.
