Design current and diversity

Every circuit design starts with one question: how much current will this circuit actually carry? The answer picks the protective device, and the device picks the cable.

Key takeaways

The design current, Ib, is the current the circuit is expected to carry in normal use: for a single-phase load, Ib = PV. Choose a protective device whose rating, In, is at least Ib, then a cable whose current-carrying capacity, Iz, is at least In once corrected for how it's installed: Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz. Diversity allows for the fact that loads rarely all run flat out together. For a household cooker, the On-Site Guide allows 10 A, plus 30% of the rest, plus 5 A if the cooker control unit has a socket: a 9.6 kW cooker drawing 41.7 A at full power can be designed for 24.5 A. Showers get no diversity.

The design process

Circuit design follows the same steps every time. This lesson covers the first two; Cable sizing and voltage drop covers the rest.

  1. Work out the design current, Ib, applying diversity where it's allowed.
  2. Choose a protective device with In ≥ Ib.
  3. Find the correction factors for the conditions: ambient temperature, grouping with other cables, thermal insulation.
  4. Work out the tabulated current the cable needs, It, and pick a cable size from the BS 7671 tables for its installation method.
  5. Check the voltage drop.
  6. Check that Zs is low enough for the device to disconnect in time (see Automatic disconnection).
  7. Check the CPC is big enough to survive the fault current until the device trips.

Design current, Ib

For a single-phase load, it's the power formula you already know (see Power):

Ib = PV

Use the rated power in watts and the nominal voltage, 230 V. An 8.5 kW shower: Ib = 8,500230 ≈ 37 A.

For a balanced three-phase load, the current in each line is Ib = P√3 × VL, with VL = 400 V. If the power factor or efficiency is known (motors, for example), divide by those too, which makes the current larger.

The device must be big enough not to trip in normal use, and the cable must be big enough that the device protects it. Breakers come in standard ratings, so pick the next one up from Ib: 6, 10, 16, 20, 25, 32, 40, 50 or 63 A. For the 37 A shower, that's a 40 A device.

There's a second condition hidden in BS 7671: the current that makes the device operate reliably, I2, must be no more than 1.45 × Iz. Circuit breakers and BS 88 fuses meet that automatically when In ≤ Iz. An old rewirable fuse (BS 3036) doesn't, so the cable has to be oversized for it by using a factor of 0.725.

Circuit breaker or RCBO?

Choosing the device also means deciding whether the circuit needs additional protection: a 30 mA RCD. It can come from an RCBO (a breaker to BS EN 61009 with the RCD built in), or from a plain circuit breaker (BS EN 60898) on a side of the board already covered by a 30 mA RCCB. A 30 mA RCD is needed for:

  • socket-outlets rated up to 32 A (regulation 411.3.3)
  • mobile equipment up to 32 A used outdoors
  • cables concealed in a wall less than 50 mm deep, unless protected another way (see Installing cables)
  • cables in walls or partitions with metal parts, at any depth
  • final circuits supplying luminaires in homes
  • every circuit in a room containing a bath or shower (see Bathrooms and special locations)

The inspection checklist in BS 7671 Appendix 6 (item 8.18 on the schedule of inspections) has a version of this list, and it's a handy quick reference. Most new domestic circuits end up with an RCD anyway, and there's nothing wrong with that. But you should know when it's required and when it's a choice.

Read the exact words. Regulation 411.3.3 applies to socket-outlets, not socket circuits. Say a customer wants one extra socket in a home whose old consumer unit has no RCD. You could spur off an existing circuit with surface wiring (in trunking, so the buried-cable rule doesn't apply) and fit a socket-outlet with its own 30 mA RCD built in. The new outlet then has its additional protection without changing the board. The lighting rule is different: 411.3.4 says circuits supplying luminaires in homes.

There's one exception to 411.3.3: outside dwellings, a documented risk assessment can decide that RCD protection isn't needed. (Older editions instead exempted sockets used under the supervision of skilled or instructed people.) BS 7671 describes who uses an installation with the codes in Appendix 5: BA1 ordinary people, BA2 children, BA3 disabled people, BA4 instructed people, BA5 skilled people. The codes help you design for the users: a school for children with additional needs may need different choices from an office.

Diversity

A cooker has four rings, a grill and an oven, but they're hardly ever all on full at once, and thermostats switch them on and off. Designing for the full rated current would mean a bigger cable and device than the circuit ever needs. Diversity is the allowance for that. The On-Site Guide (Appendix A) gives typical allowances for households:

LoadTypical allowance in a house
Cooking appliances10 A, plus 30% of the rest of the full-load current, plus 5 A if there's a socket-outlet on the cooker control unit
Instantaneous water heaters (electric showers)100% of the largest, 100% of the second largest, 25% of any others
Thermostatically controlled water heaters (immersion heaters)100%: no diversity
Lighting66% of the total demand
Socket-outlet circuits100% of the largest circuit, plus 40% of every other

These are guidance, not rules. Diversity is a judgement about how the installation will really be used, and the designer is responsible for it.
