Earthing and bonding

Automatic disconnection

The usual way BS 7671 protects you from a fault on metalwork: make the fault current big enough that the protective device switches the circuit off before anyone can be hurt.

Key takeaways

Automatic disconnection of supply (ADS) is the main method of fault protection. It needs three things working together: protective earthing (CPCs to every exposed-conductive-part), protective bonding, and a device that disconnects in time. The fault current is set by the earth fault loop impedance: Zs = Ze + (R1 + R2), and roughly I = U0Zs. On a TN system, final circuits up to 63 A with sockets (or 32 A for fixed equipment only) must disconnect within 0.4 s, and distribution circuits within 5 s. On TT the times are 0.2 s and 1 s. BS 7671 Tables 41.2 to 41.4 give the maximum Zs for each device. A measured value should be no more than about 80% of the table value, because conductors are cold when you test but warm in use.

How a fault gets cleared

Suppose the line conductor touches the metal case of an appliance. Current now flows round a loop: out from the transformer along the line conductor (R1 inside the installation), into the case, back along the CPC (R2), through the earthing system to the transformer's star point, and through the transformer winding. That loop is the earth fault loop, and its total impedance is Zs.

The part outside the installation is Ze, which depends on the earthing system (see Earthing systems). The part inside is the resistance of the circuit's own line conductor and CPC, measured together as R1 + R2:

Zs = Ze + (R1 + R2)

Ohm's law then gives the fault current: If = U0Zs, where U0 is 230 V, the voltage to earth. The lower Zs, the bigger the fault current, and the faster the device trips. To follow the loop step by step on each earthing system, open the earth fault loop explorer.

How fast is fast enough?

BS 7671 Table 41.1 sets maximum disconnection times. For a 230 V supply:

CircuitTN (TN-S, TN-C-S)TT
Final circuits up to 63 A with one or more sockets, or up to 32 A for fixed equipment only0.4 s0.2 s
Distribution circuits, and bigger final circuits5 s1 s

Sockets get the shortest time because whoever is holding a faulty appliance is gripping the metal. On TT the times are shorter still, because the voltage on exposed metalwork during a fault is higher.

Maximum Zs

For a circuit breaker to disconnect in 0.4 s, the fault current has to be big enough to operate its magnetic trip (see Consumer units and protective devices). That's 5 × In for Type B, 10 × In for Type C and 20 × In for Type D. Working backwards gives the highest Zs that still trips in time. BS 7671 also allows for the supply voltage being on the low side, using a factor Cmin of 0.95:

Zs max = U0 × CminIa

where Ia is the current that operates the device in time. For a 32 A Type B: Ia = 5 × 32 = 160 A, so Zs max = 230 × 0.95160 = 1.37 Ω. You don't need to work these out in practice: Tables 41.2 to 41.4 list them for fuses and circuit breakers.

The 80% rule. The tables apply to conductors at their working temperature, but you test them cold, when their resistance is lower. So a measured Zs is compared with about 80% of the table value (the On-Site Guide and Guidance Note 3 give corrected tables). For the 32 A Type B: 0.8 × 1.37 ≈ 1.10 Ω.

When Zs is too high

If Zs is too high for the device, you have options: a lower rating or a different type of device, a bigger cable (lower R1 + R2), or an RCD. An RCD trips on a few milliamps of imbalance, so it can provide the disconnection even when the fault current is small.

That's exactly the situation on a TT system, where Zs includes the earth electrode and the ground, and is often tens or hundreds of ohms. There, the rule is that the electrode resistance RA times the RCD's rated residual current IΔn must not be more than 50 V: RA × IΔn ≤ 50 V. For a 30 mA RCD that allows up to about 1,667 Ω, but in practice you'd want far lower, because above about 200 Ω an electrode may not be stable.
