The earth fault loop explorer

When a line conductor touches a metal case, current needs a complete loop back to the transformer. The lower the impedance of that loop, the more current flows, and the faster the breaker trips. Change the settings and watch the loop.

Key takeaways

The earth fault loop runs from the transformer winding, along the line conductor and through the breaker to the fault, back along the circuit protective conductor to the main earthing terminal, and home through the earthing system. Its impedance is Zs = Ze + (R1 + R2), and the fault current is U0Zs. Longer or thinner cables raise R1 + R2, cut the fault current, and can stop a breaker tripping in time. On TN systems a breaker can usually clear the fault; on a TT system the ground keeps the fault current to a few amps, so an RCD has to do it.

    Why it matters

    A breaker clears an earth fault quickly only when the fault current is big enough to trip it magnetically. A Type B breaker needs 5 × its rating to be sure of that: 160 A for a B32 (see Consumer units and protective devices).

    Fault current is U0Zs. Double the loop impedance and you halve the current. That's why long runs of thin cable can fail, even though nothing is wrong with them.

    On a TT system the loop goes through the ground, so Zs is tens or hundreds of ohms. The fault current is a few amps, far too small to trip a breaker. An RCD does the job instead: it trips on as little as 30 mA of imbalance.

    On site, you measure Ze at the origin with the earthing conductor disconnected, and R1 + R2 during dead testing. You can then check a live Zs reading against Ze + (R1 + R2) (see Live tests).
