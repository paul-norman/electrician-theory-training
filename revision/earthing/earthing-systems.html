Earthing systems

Every installation needs a connection to earth, so that a fault on metalwork can be cleared quickly. Where that connection comes from is the earthing system, and it changes how you design, test and protect.

Key takeaways

The letters describe the earth connections. The first letter is the supply: T means the transformer's star point is connected directly to earth. The second is the installation: N means its earth comes from the supply's neutral or earth conductor, T means it has its own earth electrode. In TN-S the supply provides a separate earth conductor, usually the cable's metal sheath (typical maximum Ze 0.8 Ω). In TN-C-S, also called PME, the supply cable combines neutral and earth in one PEN conductor, earthed at several points along the network, and they're separated at the cut-out (typical maximum Ze 0.35 Ω). It's the most common system in UK homes. In TT the supply gives no earth at all: the installation has its own earth rod, the earth fault loop impedance is high, and RCDs do the fault protection. Amendment 4 of BS 7671 adds PNB, a form of TN-C-S where neutral and earth are joined at a single point near the installation.

Why the earth connection matters

If a line conductor touches a metal case, that case becomes live. With a good earth connection, the fault current flows through the circuit protective conductor (CPC), back through the earthing system to the transformer, and is big enough to trip the circuit's protective device in a fraction of a second. The path it takes is the earth fault loop, and its impedance decides how big the fault current is (see Automatic disconnection).

The part of that loop outside the installation is the external earth fault loop impedance, Ze. It depends almost entirely on the earthing system, which is why the system has to be identified and recorded on every certificate.

Where the earth comes from

TN-S: a separate earth from the supply

The supply cable carries a separate protective conductor all the way from the transformer. In older underground supplies it's the cable's lead sheath or steel armour, and you'll see the earthing conductor clamped to the sheath where the cable enters the cut-out. The DNO typically quotes a maximum Ze of 0.8 Ω.

TN-S was the standard for decades, so it's common in older urban houses. As old sheaths corrode, the earth path can deteriorate, which is one reason Ze is measured rather than assumed.

TN-C-S (PME): neutral and earth combined in the supply

The supply cable has a single PEN conductor (protective earth and neutral) instead of separate neutral and earth. The DNO connects it to earth at the transformer and at several more points along the network: that's the "multiple earthing" in protective multiple earthing (PME). At the cut-out the PEN is split: the neutral goes on to the meter, and an earth terminal, connected to the neutral inside the cut-out, provides the installation's earth. From there on, neutral and earth are separate (the "S" in TN-C-S). The usual quoted maximum Ze is 0.35 Ω.

PME is now the most common arrangement for UK homes. Its weakness is the PEN conductor. If it breaks upstream, the installation's earth is connected to the neutral of a circuit with no return path, so all the earthed metalwork, and anything bonded to it, can rise towards 230 V. That's why:

  • main protective bonding on a PME supply is sized from the supply neutral, and is usually 10 mm² in a house (see Protective bonding)
  • the ESQCR doesn't allow a PME earth to be provided to caravans or boats
  • BS 7671 has extra rules, or doesn't allow a PME earth, for some outdoor and special locations, such as many EV charge points outdoors, construction sites and some agricultural buildings. In those cases a TT earth is often used for that part of the installation.

TT: the installation provides its own earth

The supply provides no earth at all, typically on rural overhead lines. The installation's MET is connected to its own earth electrode, usually a copper-clad steel rod driven into the ground. A fault current has to travel through the ground back to the transformer's own earth, and soil is a poor conductor, so the earth fault loop impedance is many ohms.

That makes the fault current far too small to trip a circuit breaker quickly. So on a TT system the fault protection normally comes from RCDs, which only need a few milliamps of imbalance. Guidance Note 3 advises that an electrode resistance above about 200 Ω may not be stable, because it changes as the ground dries out or freezes.

PNB: protective neutral bonding

Amendment 4 of BS 7671 (2026) adds requirements and drawings for protective neutral bonding (PNB). It's a form of TN-C-S where the neutral and earth are connected together, and to earth, at one point only, at or near the consumer's installation, rather than at many points along the network. You'll typically meet it where a single customer, such as a farm or a large house, is fed from its own transformer.

You need to recognise a PNB supply and know it isn't the same as PME, because it can change the earthing arrangement, the risk assessment and which equipment is suitable. The details are in Part 3 of BS 7671 (regulation 312.2.1.1 and its figures).

The supply cables

The DNO's cable is often the clue to the earthing system:

  • Concentric cable (TN-C-S): a single central conductor (the line), surrounded by a ring of strands that form the combined neutral and earth (PEN). At the cut-out the strands are twisted together onto the neutral terminal, and the earth terminal is connected to it. It can look a little like steel wire armoured cable, but it isn't.
  • Paper-insulated lead-covered cable (TN-S): older cable with paper-insulated cores, a lead sheath and a bumpy outer covering of bitumen-soaked tape. The lead sheath is the earth path back to the transformer, and the earthing conductor is clamped or soldered (wiped) onto it.
  • TT: either kind of cable, but the earth isn't used, because the supply doesn't provide one.

Whatever the system, every installation has one main earthing terminal (MET). It can be the earth bar inside the consumer unit, or a separate terminal block next to it. The earthing conductor, the main protective bonding conductors and (through the consumer unit) every circuit's CPC all meet there.

Appearances can mislead: sheaths get replaced, and labels go missing. If in doubt, ask the DNO, and always measure Ze (see Live tests). Never connect an installation's earth to a gas or water pipe: pipes aren't an earth electrode, and plastic sections can break the path without anyone knowing.
