Protective bonding

Earthing gives fault current a path home. Bonding makes sure that, while a fault is being cleared, nothing you can touch at the same time is at a dangerously different voltage.

Key takeaways

An exposed-conductive-part is metal belonging to the electrical installation that could become live in a fault, such as the case of a Class I appliance: it's earthed through the CPC. An extraneous-conductive-part isn't part of the electrical installation but can bring in a potential, usually earth, from outside, such as a metal gas or water pipe: it's bonded. Main protective bonding connects incoming metal services to the MET, as close as practical to where they enter the building (for gas, within 600 mm of the meter on the consumer's side, before any branch). In a typical house the earthing conductor is 16 mm² and the main bonding is 10 mm². Supplementary bonding links parts within one location that someone could touch at the same time. It's usually 4 mm² (2.5 mm² if mechanically protected), and in a bathroom it can be left out when every circuit has 30 mA RCD protection and main bonding is in place.

Why bond?

When a fault puts 230 V on a metal case, the fault current flows through the CPC and the earthing system. That path has some impedance, so for the short time before the breaker trips, the case sits at a raised voltage above true earth. Now imagine a metal water pipe nearby, connected to the ground outside. Someone touching the case and the pipe at the same time would have the full difference across their body.

Bonding connects the pipe to the same earthing point as the case. Both then rise together during the fault, so the voltage between them stays low. The aim isn't to carry fault current; it's to keep everything you can touch at roughly the same potential. That's why it's called equipotential bonding.

Exposed and extraneous parts

Exposed-conductive-partExtraneous-conductive-part
What it isMetal that's part of the electrical equipment, not normally live, but could become live if the basic insulation fails.Metal that isn't part of the electrical installation, but could introduce a potential (usually earth) into the building.
ExamplesThe metal case of a Class I appliance, metal back boxes, steel conduit and trunking, a metal consumer unit.Metal water, gas and oil pipes entering the building, exposed structural steel, metal ducting and some central heating pipework.
Connected byThe circuit protective conductor (CPC)Main protective bonding conductors (and sometimes supplementary bonding)

The test for "extraneous" is whether the metal is liable to introduce a potential. A metal sink fed entirely by plastic pipes is isolated, so it isn't extraneous and doesn't need bonding. Bonding things that don't need it can actually make matters worse, by bringing an earth potential to a place where there wasn't one. If you're not sure, measure the resistance from the metal to the MET: Guidance Note 3 explains how to judge the result.

Main protective bonding

Main protective bonding conductors run from the MET to each extraneous-conductive-part that enters the building:

  • metal water and gas pipes, and oil supply pipes
  • other metal service pipes and ducting
  • exposed metallic structural parts of the building
  • a lightning protection system, where the lightning protection designer says so

Make the connection as close as practical to where the service enters the building. For gas, it goes on the consumer's side of the meter, within 600 mm of the meter's outlet union, and before any branch in the pipework (or at the point of entry, if the meter is outside). Use a proper earth clamp (to BS 951) with its permanent label: "Safety Electrical Connection – Do Not Remove".

Conductor sizes in a typical house

The sizes come from BS 7671 Chapter 54. For a house with 25 mm² meter tails, the answers are almost always the same:

ConductorTypical sizeHow it's worked out
Earthing conductor (supply earth to MET)16 mm²Related to the size of the line conductors: for tails over 16 mm² and up to 35 mm², a 16 mm² copper conductor is enough.
Main protective bonding, TN-S or TT10 mm²At least half the size of the earthing conductor, and never less than 6 mm².
Main protective bonding, TN-C-S (PME)10 mm²Set by the supply neutral instead: with a supply neutral up to 35 mm², 10 mm² copper. Bigger supplies need more.
Supplementary bonding4 mm²4 mm² if not mechanically protected, 2.5 mm² if it is (for example, run inside conduit). Never smaller than the CPCs it connects, where it joins exposed-conductive-parts.

Bonding and earthing conductors are always green and yellow. Amendment 4 of BS 7671 also covers functional earthing and bonding for data and network equipment (Section 545). Those conductors serve the equipment, not safety, so they mustn't use green and yellow.

Supplementary bonding

Supplementary bonding is extra, local bonding. It connects exposed- and extraneous-conductive-parts that someone could touch at the same time in one location, so that they stay at the same potential even if a fault isn't cleared as quickly as hoped. You'll see it where the risk is higher or where disconnection times can't be met.

Bathrooms used to need it as standard. Under the current rules it can be left out of a room containing a bath or shower when all three of these are true:

  • every circuit in the location has 30 mA RCD protection
  • every circuit in the location meets the disconnection times for automatic disconnection
  • the extraneous-conductive-parts in the location are connected to the main protective bonding

See Bathrooms and special locations for the rest of the bathroom rules.
