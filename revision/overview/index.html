Becoming an electrician in the UK

What "qualified" means, the different routes to it, and where this course and its six exams fit in.

Key takeaways

To be recognised as a fully qualified electrician in the UK you normally need three things: a Level 3 competence qualification (an NVQ or its replacement, built on evidence of real work on site), a pass in the AM2 practical assessment (or one of its variants), and a current 18th Edition (BS 7671) qualification. The ECS Gold Card is the industry's proof of that status. Separately, to sign off your own notifiable work in homes you join a Competent Person Scheme such as NICEIC or NAPIT. Short courses like the theory exams here are a big part of the journey, but on their own they aren't the whole qualification.

What "qualified" means

There's no single licence to be an electrician in the UK, and anyone can legally do some electrical work. But the industry, employers and customers recognise a standard set of qualifications:

  • A Level 3 competence qualification. Traditionally the Level 3 NVQ Diploma in Installing Electrotechnical Systems and Equipment. Newer versions include the Level 3 Electrotechnical Qualification and, for homes only, the Level 3 Electrotechnical in Dwellings. They combine knowledge with a portfolio of evidence from real installation work.
  • The AM2, a practical end assessment run by NET at approved centres: a couple of days of installing, inspecting, testing and fault-finding under exam conditions. There are versions for each route, such as AM2S for apprentices, AM2E for experienced workers and AM2D for domestic electricians.
  • A current 18th Edition qualification, showing you know the Wiring Regulations.

With those, you can apply for an ECS Gold Card (Electrotechnical Certification Scheme), which most contractors and sites ask for. There's an Installation Electrician card and a Domestic Electrician card. You'll also need to pass the ECS health, safety and environmental assessment.

The routes

RouteWho it's forWhat's involved
ApprenticeshipUsually school leavers employed by an electrical firmAbout 3 to 4 years: college plus work on site, ending with an end-point assessment that includes the AM2S. There's an Installation and Maintenance Electrician standard and a Domestic Electrician standard.
Adult learnerCareer changersKnowledge qualifications (Level 2 and 3 diplomas, or a provider's equivalent), then a Level 3 competence qualification with an on-site portfolio, then the AM2. Needs real site work, often as an improver or mate.
Experienced workerPeople already doing electrical work, usually for several years, without the formal qualificationsThe Experienced Worker Assessment (EWA): evidence of past work plus assessments, then the AM2E.
Domestic installerPeople working only in homesShort courses (such as Domestic Electrical Installer, 18th Edition, Building Regulations and inspection and testing) and registration with a Competent Person Scheme to self-certify Part P work. Quicker, but limited to domestic work, and not the full qualification.

Names and details change. EAL, the awarding body for this course's exams, has been updating its Level 3 pathways for non-apprentice learners during 2026. Always check the current requirements with your training provider, NET (for the AM2) and the JIB (for the ECS card).

  3. Pass the practical assessmentsInspecting and testing a real board, and completing the certificates. The inspection and testing and PAT qualifications each include a practical.
  4. Get site experience and build your portfolioWork on real installations, usually alongside a qualified electrician, collecting evidence for your Level 3 competence qualification (the NVQ or its replacement). Your assessor checks the evidence, so do everything by the book: safe isolation, never touching the DNO's cut-out.
  5. Pass the AM2The practical end assessment at a NET-approved centre, in the version for your route.
  6. Get your ECS Gold Card, and register to self-certifyApply for the ECS card. If you'll work for yourself in homes, join a Competent Person Scheme (such as NICEIC or NAPIT), which assesses your work and lets you notify building control yourself (see Part P and notification).
  7. Keep up to dateBS 7671 is amended every few years (Amendment 4 arrived in 2026), and schemes expect you to keep your knowledge current.
