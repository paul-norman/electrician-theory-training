In-service inspection and testing

Often called PAT, this is the checking of electrical equipment while it's in use: kettles, drills and extension leads, but also fixed and IT equipment. The reference is the IET Code of Practice, 5th edition.

Key takeaways

No law says "test every year". The Electricity at Work Regulations and PUWER require equipment to be maintained so it isn't dangerous, and the duty holder decides how often by risk assessment. The Code of Practice covers all electrical equipment, not just portable appliances. There are three levels: a user check before each use, a formal visual inspection (FVI) by a competent person, and a combined inspection and test (CIT). Most faults are found by looking. Class I equipment relies on earthed metalwork; Class II relies on double or reinforced insulation (the double-square symbol) and has no earth. The tests, in order: protective conductor continuity (Class I, limit 0.1 Ω plus the cord's own resistance), insulation resistance at 500 V DC (at least 1.0 MΩ for Class I, 2.0 MΩ for Class II), protective conductor or touch current if needed, then a functional check.

The law and the Code of Practice

The duties come from general health and safety law, not a "PAT law":

  • The Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 require electrical systems, including equipment, to be maintained to prevent danger, so far as is reasonably practicable.
  • PUWER requires work equipment to be kept in efficient working order and good repair.
  • The Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 require a risk assessment, which sets how often equipment is checked.
  • Landlords must keep equipment they supply with a tenancy safe, checking it periodically and at each new tenancy.

Records aren't specifically required by law, but the HSE recommends keeping them, and they're the evidence that maintenance was done. The IET Code of Practice for In-service Inspection and Testing of Electrical Equipment (5th edition) explains how to do it, and it's the book allowed in the exam. Because it covers every type of equipment, it avoids the term "portable appliance".

Equipment classes

The class tells you how the equipment protects against electric shock, and so which tests apply:

Class I Basic insulation, plus accessible metal parts connected to earth through the plug's earth pin. Always a 3-core flex. If the insulation fails, the fault goes to earth and the fuse or breaker disconnects it.
Class II Double or reinforced insulation, with no earth connection, so it relies on the insulation alone. Marked with the double square. Often a 2-core flex. Class II FE has a functional earth that isn't for safety.
Class III Supplied at a safe extra-low voltage from a source such as a safety isolating transformer. The 5th edition describes these supplies with energy source classes (ES1 to ES3) rather than SELV.

There's no fixed timescale. Frequency depends on the environment, the user, how the equipment is built and used, and its history: a drill on a building site needs checking far more often than a printer in an office. The Code of Practice gives example starting points.

The formal visual inspection

Before inspecting, check you're allowed to switch the equipment off and unplug it (a server or freezer may not be), then disconnect it. Look at:

  • The environment and suitability: is it right for where and how it's used? Is there room for ventilation? Are cables a trip hazard?
  • The plug: no cracks or burn marks; sleeved pins (required on plugs since 1984); the right fuse. As a guide, equipment up to about 700 W takes a 3 A fuse; above that, 13 A. Inside: the cord grip holding the sheath, correct connections, tight terminals.
  • The flex: no cuts, damage, joints or taped repairs, and suitable for the job.
  • The equipment: no damage to the case, no overheating, all covers and screws in place.

Extension leads aren't a permanent extension of the fixed wiring, so inspect them more often. Maximum lengths: 12 m for 1.25 mm² flex, 15 m for 1.5 mm². Longer leads need a 30 mA RCD.

The tests

  1. Protective conductor continuity (Class I only). Proves accessible metal is connected to the plug's earth pin. Flex the cord along its length during the test, to find broken strands. The limit is (0.1 + R) Ω, where R is the resistance of the cord's protective conductor; 0.1 Ω where there's no cord. A low test current (20 to 200 mA) suits most equipment; a high current (up to 25 A) needs a solid connection to bare metal.
  2. Insulation resistance. 500 V DC between the live conductors (joined together) and earth, with the equipment switched on. Minimum 1.0 MΩ for Class I and 2.0 MΩ for Class II. For equipment with electronics that a 500 V test could damage or upset, test at 250 V, or use the protective conductor or touch current test instead.
  3. Protective conductor current or touch current, where needed. The equipment runs normally while the tester measures the current flowing in its earth (Class I), or that would flow through a person touching it.
  4. Functional check. Switch it on and make sure it works properly. Fill a kettle first, take care with moving parts, and don't touch metal parts while it's running.

Extension leads and cord sets are tested as items on their own: earth continuity, insulation resistance and polarity for a 3-core lead; insulation and polarity for a 2-core one.

Practice

Earth continuity limits

Protective conductor resistance per metre: 0.75 mm² 26 mΩ, 1.0 mm² 19.5 mΩ, 1.25 mm² 15.6 mΩ, 1.5 mm² 13.3 mΩ.

Records and labels

The Code of Practice gives model forms: an equipment register (make, model, serial or asset number, inspection and test frequency, repairs), an inspection and test record for each visit, a faulty equipment register and a repair register. Labels show that an item has been checked. Test instruments must be to BS EN 61010 with GS38 leads, and their accuracy should be checked regularly, since a calibration certificate only proves the instrument was accurate on the day.
