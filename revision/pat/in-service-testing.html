In-service inspection and testing

Often called PAT, this is the checking of electrical equipment while it's in use: kettles, drills and extension leads, but also fixed and IT equipment. The reference is the IET Code of Practice, 5th edition.

Key takeaways

No law says "test every year". The Electricity at Work Regulations and PUWER require equipment to be maintained so it isn't dangerous, and the duty holder decides how often by risk assessment. The Code of Practice covers all electrical equipment, not just portable appliances. There are three levels: a user check before each use, a formal visual inspection (FVI) by a competent person, and a combined inspection and test (CIT). Most faults are found by looking. Class I equipment relies on earthed metalwork; Class II relies on double or reinforced insulation (the double-square symbol) and has no earth. The tests, in order: protective conductor continuity (Class I, limit 0.1 Ω plus the cord's own resistance), insulation resistance at 500 V DC (at least 1.0 MΩ for Class I, 2.0 MΩ for Class II), protective conductor or touch current if needed, then a functional check. Anything without the Class II mark is treated as Class I, and an all-plastic extension lead is Class I. Labels show the date, initials, item number and status, never a next test date. Tests go in order: continuity (Class I and II FE), insulation resistance with line and neutral joined, touch current if needed, functional check. RCDs built into leads and adaptors must trip within 40 ms at 150 mA.

The law and the Code of Practice

The duties come from general health and safety law, not a "PAT law":

  • The Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 require electrical systems, including equipment, to be maintained to prevent danger, so far as is reasonably practicable.
  • PUWER requires work equipment to be kept in efficient working order and good repair.
  • The Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 require a risk assessment, which sets how often equipment is checked.
  • Landlords must keep equipment they supply with a tenancy safe, checking it periodically and at each new tenancy. The Housing Acts set this out: the Housing Act 2004 (England and Wales), the Housing (Scotland) Act 2006 and the Housing (Wales) Act 2014.

Section 3 of the Code of Practice lists the legislation, and a few details come up in exams:

  • The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 sits at the top; the regulations above are made under it.
  • The Management Regulations (regulation 3(1)) require a suitable and sufficient risk assessment covering employees and anyone else affected, such as visitors to a site.
  • PUWER covers work equipment only, not the fixed installation. Regulation 4 requires equipment suitable for its purpose, regulation 5 keeps it maintained, and maintenance records are expected. Meeting the Electricity at Work Regulations will usually meet PUWER too.
  • The Electricity at Work Regulations cover everything from a 400 kV overhead line to a battery lamp. "Danger" means risk of injury, including death. Table 3.1 summarises regulations 4 to 16 (below).
  • Also listed: the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016, the Supply of Machinery (Safety) Regulations 2008, the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982 and the Plugs and Sockets etc. (Safety) Regulations 1994.

Records aren't specifically required by law, but the HSE recommends keeping them, and they're the evidence that maintenance was done. The IET Code of Practice for In-service Inspection and Testing of Electrical Equipment (5th edition) explains how to do it, and it's the book allowed in the exam. Because it covers every type of equipment, it avoids the term "portable appliance".

What the Code of Practice covers

It covers all electrical equipment connected to the fixed wiring, not just things with a plug. For years "PAT" meant "anything with a plug top", and hard-wired equipment such as hand dryers and wall heaters on fused connection units was never checked. That was wrong. Section 4 lists the kinds of equipment in scope:

  • Mobile: desk lamps, toasters, fan heaters
  • Stationary: welders, bench grinders, IT racks, vending machines
  • Fixed: lighting, hand dryers, boilers, alarms and security panels, split air conditioning units
  • Handheld: hair dryers, floor polishers, power tools
  • Built-in appliances: dishwashers, fridges, ovens, hobs
  • IT equipment: laptop power supplies, monitors, computers, printers
  • Extension leads, multiway adaptors and RCD adaptors
  • EV charging equipment not under another maintenance regime, electric heating (storage heaters, immersion heaters) and electrical installations in office furniture

These are descriptions, not a classification you record: the old categories (portable, movable, stationary and so on) are no longer used on the forms.

It applies wherever equipment is used for work or supplied to others: offices, shops, hotels, schools, hospitals, industrial sites, construction sites, portable buildings, caravans (static and touring), swimming pools and farms. A home is in scope only where someone else is responsible for the equipment: a landlord's appliances in a rented house or a holiday caravan, or equipment used for a business at home, such as a childminder's playroom or a home office, which comes under PUWER. Your own kettle in your own house isn't covered.

Hard-wired equipment takes longer to test: you identify and isolate the circuit, disconnect the appliance, and connect it temporarily so the tester can reach it. Agree the scope and the price with the duty holder first. They may give you their equipment register and ask you to test only what's on it.

New, second-hand, hired and fake equipment (Section 13)

  • New equipment shouldn't normally need testing before it goes into service, but it should be added to the asset register (equipment register) and get a user check.
  • Second-hand equipment sold for profit must be safe and should be inspected and tested.
  • Hired equipment becomes the hirer's responsibility, unless there's a maintenance contract with the supplier.
  • Counterfeit equipment carries fake marks that are hard to spot: Figure 13.1 shows a real and a fake BSI Kitemark. Genuine conformity marks include UKCA (introduced in Great Britain after Brexit) and CE.

Two definitions worth knowing

An accessible metal part is any metal part that can be touched with a standard test finger (BS EN 61032), or that's connected to such a part. Appendix 2 of the Code of Practice has the full IP code table (first digit solids, second water, plus the additional letters) and the IK impact codes. It's a useful revision page for every exam.

Equipment classes

The class tells you how the equipment protects against electric shock, and so which tests apply:

Class I Basic insulation, plus accessible metal parts connected to earth through the plug's earth pin. Always a 3-core flex. If the insulation fails, the fault goes to earth and the fuse or breaker disconnects it.
Class II Double or reinforced insulation, with no earth connection, so it relies on the insulation alone. Marked with the double square. Often a 2-core flex. Class II FE has a functional earth that isn't for safety.
Class III Supplied at a safe extra-low voltage from a source such as a safety isolating transformer. The 5th edition describes these supplies with energy source classes (ES1 to ES3) rather than SELV.

Some details from Section 6 and the definitions:

  • Class I can include unearthed metal that's separated from live parts by basic and supplementary insulation, such as the soleplate of some irons or the fins of an oil-filled radiator. A plastic kettle is still Class I: its element is earthed.
  • Class II can be metal: some metal light fittings are Class II, which matters on old lighting circuits with no CPC.
  • Class II FE: electronic equipment (laptops, TVs, games consoles) turns the AC supply into DC with a rectifier, and filter capacitors leak a little current to earth. The earth connection carries that leakage; it isn't there for shock protection.
  • Class 0: basic insulation only, with no earth and no second layer, so a crack in the insulation leaves a live part exposed. Old mains series-wired Christmas tree lights are the classic example. It's no longer allowed.
  • Energy source classes (definitions and Appendix 3): ES1 is detectable but not painful, within 30 V AC RMS or 60 V DC; ES2 exceeds ES1 but stays within the ES2 limits; ES3 is anything beyond. RMS is 0.707 × the peak value, so 230 V RMS peaks at about 325 V.
  • Table 6.1 shows the symbols. If equipment doesn't show the Class II mark, treat it as Class I. A flat two-core flex or a plastic earth pin on a moulded plug hints at Class II, but the symbol is what counts.
  • An extension lead is Class I even if it's all plastic: its sockets carry an earth, and anything could be plugged into it.

There's no fixed timescale. Frequency depends on the environment, the user, how the equipment is built and used, and its history: a drill on a building site needs checking far more often than a printer in an office. The Code of Practice gives example starting points.

Section 11 lists what decides the frequency: the environment, the users (do they look after it?), the construction (robust Class I or more fragile Class II), the type of equipment (handheld, fixed and so on), how often it's used, how it's installed, its records and history, and its intended service life (some equipment, with built-in batteries or software, is meant to be replaced after a few years). The duty holder carries out the risk assessment and tells the inspector the frequency, not the other way round. Appendix 9 has an example risk assessment with suggested intervals for workshop tools, office and kitchen equipment and the like. It's guidance, not a rule.

Who can inspect and test (Section 5)

Section 5 covers training and competence for users, inspectors, test operatives and duty holders. It describes two levels:

  • A level 1 person isn't skilled in electrical work, and routinely uses a simple pass/fail instrument where no readings need interpreting, following set procedures with clear acceptance criteria.
  • A level 2 person has electrical skills and uses more sophisticated instruments whose readings need interpreting. That's what you'll be doing with a multifunction tester on fixed wiring.

The formal visual inspection

Before inspecting, check you're allowed to switch the equipment off and unplug it (a server or freezer may not be), and ask the user to save their work and power down, so no data is lost. For hard-wired equipment, find out what else is on the circuit before you isolate it. Then disconnect it. Section 8 lists what to look at:

  • The environment and suitability: is it right for where and how it's used? Is there room for ventilation? Are cables a trip hazard?
  • The plug: no cracks or burn marks; sleeved pins (required on plugs since 1984); the right fuse. As a guide, equipment up to about 700 W takes a 3 A fuse; above that, 13 A. Inside: the cord grip holding the outer sheath, with no inner insulation showing below it; correct connections; tight terminals; no loose strands or bare copper showing. Table 10.6 gives the right fuse for each flex size.
  • The flex: no cuts, damage, joints or taped repairs, and suitable for the job.
  • The equipment: no damage to the case, no overheating, all covers and screws in place.

Appendix 6 illustrates common faults found on plugs and flexes. Some worth knowing:

  • The earth pin is longer than the others so it opens the socket's shutters first; the line and neutral pins are sleeved so no bare metal can be touched while the plug is part-way in.
  • A plug with a plastic earth pin is only for Class II equipment, and the earth pin itself is never sleeved.
  • The fitting instructions card must be removed, and the fuse must sit properly in its holder (never bridged with foil).
  • Not allowed: two flexes in one plug, twin and earth cable instead of flex, a split sheath, or a terminal screw sheared off (cut the plug off and fit a new one).
  • Round-pin plugs (5 A, 15 A) have no fuse, so they rely on the circuit's protection.

Extension leads aren't a permanent extension of the fixed wiring, so inspect them more often. Maximum lengths: 12 m for 1.25 mm² flex, 15 m for 1.5 mm². Longer leads need a 30 mA RCD (Table 10.3), because the longer cable's higher resistance means less fault current. Unwind a reel fully to inspect it. Every core of the flex must be connected, matching the plug's terminals. If a room relies on lots of extension leads, recommend to the duty holder that more fixed socket-outlets are installed.

Polarity means every conductor is on the right terminal: brown to L, blue to N, green and yellow to earth. Fuses and single-pole switches go in the line conductor only; a neutral may be switched only by a device that switches the line at the same time, such as a double-pole switch. On a desk lamp with an Edison screw (ES) lampholder, the centre contact must be the line and the screw thread the neutral. Reversed, the thread you touch while changing a lamp would be live.

The tests

After the formal visual inspection, the tests are done in this order:

  1. Protective conductor continuity (Class I and Class II FE only: Class II has no protective conductor to test). Proves accessible metal is connected to the plug's earth pin. Flex the cord along its length during the test, to find broken strands. The limit is (0.1 + R) Ω, where R is the resistance of the cord's protective conductor; 0.1 Ω where there's no cord. A low test current (20 to 200 mA) suits most equipment; a high current (up to 25 A) needs a solid connection to bare metal and should only be applied long enough to get a stable reading. Clip onto a screw head or other bare metal; never scratch through paint or powder coating to get a reading. For older equipment, a higher reading can be accepted if the inspector is satisfied it's down to the design and not a deteriorating earth (note to Table 10.1).
  2. Insulation resistance. 500 V DC between the live conductors (joined together) and earth, with the equipment switched on. Minimum 1.0 MΩ for Class I and 2.0 MΩ for Class II. For equipment with electronics that a 500 V test could damage or upset, test at 250 V, or use the protective conductor or touch current test instead.
  3. Protective conductor current or touch current, where needed. The equipment runs normally while the tester measures the current flowing in its earth (Class I or II FE), or that would flow through a person touching it. Read it once it has been stable for more than 2 seconds; it shouldn't exceed 5 mA for mains-powered equipment.
  4. Functional check. Switch it on and make sure it works properly. Fill a kettle first, take care with moving parts, and don't touch metal parts while it's running. Some testers can also do a load test, which is useful for heating equipment.

Extension leads and cord sets are tested as items on their own: earth continuity, insulation resistance and polarity for a 3-core lead; insulation and polarity for a 2-core one. The lead plugs into the tester, and a test lead (usually red, labelled for test use only) goes from the tester into one of its sockets, making a loop. Test every socket on a multiway lead, since any one could be faulty.

Why line and neutral are joined for the insulation test

The equipment is connected, so its load sits between line and neutral. Test between them and the tester's voltage simply flows through the element or motor: you'd read almost nothing, and a 500 V test could damage electronics. So the tester joins line and neutral internally and tests from both of them to earth. Both are then at the same test voltage, so nothing flows through the load, and any current that does flow must be leaking through damaged insulation to the earthed parts.

RCDs in equipment and leads

RCD adaptors, plugs with a built-in RCD and extension leads with one are in scope and must be tested. They need a supply, so plug them into a socket, press the test button (it should trip), then, in a combined inspection and test, do a performance test with an instrument: at 5 × IΔn (150 mA for a 30 mA device) it must trip within 40 ms (Table 10.5). Don't confuse this with fixed wiring, where the recorded test is at 1 × IΔn (30 mA), with a 300 ms limit. Watch out for an RCD upstream: if the socket you're using is already RCD protected, the test may trip that one instead, switching off other people's equipment and proving nothing about the device you're testing. Use a socket without RCD protection where you can.

Records and labels

The IET model forms are numbered V1 to V6 (V1 is the equipment register, V2 the formal visual and combined inspection and test record, V3 the labels, V4 the repair register, and the last the test instrument record). Appendix 4 of the book still shows only two of them, under older numbers, so note the V numbers on a sticky note in Appendix 4. The test record holds the item's ID, description, class, location, the frequencies set by the duty holder, make, model and serial number, voltage if not 230 V, rating, the visual results (plug or socket, flex, body), the test results (continuity, or N/A for Class II; insulation resistance; polarity; functional check), whether it's suitable for its environment and continued use, and your initials. Note any fault you spot in the fixed installation too, such as a damaged socket behind an appliance. HSE guidance HSG107, Maintaining portable electrical equipment, is also good revision.

The Code of Practice gives model forms: an equipment register (make, model, serial or asset number, inspection and test frequency, repairs), an inspection and test record for each visit, a faulty equipment register and a repair register. Labels show that an item has been checked: the date, the tester's initials and the item's unique number (the Code of Practice shows examples), with a pass or fail status. Don't put a next test date on the label. The duty holder decides how often each item is checked, by risk assessment, because only they know where it will be used next. Your job is to report its condition on the day. Testers range from simple pass/fail units, through mid-range ones that store results and do touch current and RCD tests, to models with label printers and barcode scanners; a multifunction tester with a PAT adaptor also works. Any adaptors or leads made up for testing must be labelled for test use only. Test instruments must be to BS EN 61010 with GS38 leads, and their accuracy should be checked regularly, since a calibration certificate only proves the instrument was accurate on the day.
