Atoms and free electrons

Everything in this trade comes down to tiny charged particles moving through metal. Here's what's actually happening inside a copper wire.

Key takeaways

Metals conduct because each atom gives up an outer electron to a shared "sea" of free electrons, which can move through a fixed lattice of positive ions. The electrons always move quickly at random. A voltage adds a slow drift in one direction, and that drift is the current. Insulators like PVC have no free electrons, so no current flows. Heat makes the ions vibrate harder and get in the electrons' way, so a hot conductor has more resistance.

A quick picture of an atom

An atom has a small, heavy nucleus in the middle, made of protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge). Around it are much lighter electrons (negative charge).

A normal atom has the same number of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel out and the atom has no overall charge. Two rules about charge explain a lot of what follows:

  • Opposite charges attract (+ pulls −).
  • Like charges repel (− pushes −).

Why metals conduct

In a metal, the atoms pack together in a neat, repeating grid called a crystal lattice. Each atom lets go of its outermost electron (its valence electron). Those electrons aren't tied to any one atom. They form a shared "sea" that can wander through the whole piece of metal.

The atoms left behind are now short of an electron, so each is a positive ion. They stay fixed in the lattice. Only the electrons move.

Copper has one free electron per atom. That's about 85,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 free electrons in every cubic metre (8.5 × 1028). A wire is packed full of charge that's ready to move.

What the animation shows

  • No battery: electrons move fast but randomly. No overall flow means no current.
  • Battery connected: the + terminal attracts electrons and the − terminal repels them. Each electron gets a small extra push to the left, on top of its random motion. That slow overall drift is the current.
  • Hotter: the ions vibrate harder, and electrons bump into them more often. Each bump wastes some of the push, so fewer get through. Hot metal has more resistance. You'll meet this again when testing circuits: a cable's resistance at its working temperature (around 70 °C) is about 20% higher than when it's cold.
  • PVC: each electron stays tied to its own atom. The battery can nudge them slightly, but they can't travel. No free electrons means no current: an insulator.

Nothing is a perfect insulator. Push the voltage high enough and even air breaks down: that's a spark, or lightning. That's why insulation is tested with high voltages (hundreds of volts) during inspection and testing.

Surprisingly slow

With 1 A flowing in a 1.5 mm² cable, the electrons drift at about 0.05 mm per second. That's slower than a snail. So why does the light come on instantly? The wire is already full of electrons. Push at one end and every electron along the wire starts moving at once, like a tube packed full of marbles.
