Electrical principles

DC and AC

Direct current flows one way; alternating current keeps reversing. Both turn up in your work, and they behave differently in ways that affect safety and equipment choice.

Key takeaways

Direct current (DC) flows one way at a steady voltage; it comes from batteries and solar panels, and electronics run on it inside. Alternating current (AC) reverses direction continuously: UK mains is a 50 Hz sine wave, so one cycle (360°, or 2π radians) takes 20 ms. "230 V" is the RMS (effective) value: the DC voltage that would heat a resistor the same amount. The actual peaks are about ±325 V (230 × √2). A rectifier turns AC into DC; an inverter turns DC into AC; transformers only work on AC. DC needs extra care: with no zero crossings its arcs are hard to break, so switches and isolators must be DC-rated, and smooth DC leakage can stop some RCDs working. RCD types run AC, A, F and B; car chargers and some solar and battery systems need DC fault detection.

Direct current (DC)

With direct current, the voltage stays at a steady value and polarity, so the current always flows the same way round the circuit, from + to −. Plotted against time it's a flat line.

Where you'll meet DC:

  • Batteries and cells: torches, test instruments, smoke alarm backups, emergency lighting.
  • Solar photovoltaic (PV) panels, which generate DC, and home battery storage.
  • Electric vehicles: the car battery is DC. Rapid chargers supply DC straight to it; home chargers supply AC and the car converts it.
  • Electronics: phones, computers, LED lamps and controls all run on DC internally, converted from the mains by a power supply or driver.

DC and AC over time

Over 40 ms, two cycles of 50 Hz mains. The DC line never changes. The AC wave crosses zero 100 times a second, and each time it does, any arc across opening contacts tends to go out by itself. DC never gives an arc that chance.

Alternating current (AC)

The mains supply is alternating current: the voltage swings positive, back through zero, negative, and back again, so the current keeps reversing direction. In the UK it completes 50 cycles a second, a frequency of 50 hertz (Hz). So one cycle takes T = 1f = 150 = 0.02 s, or 20 ms.

The shape is a sine wave, because it comes from a spinning generator. One full cycle is one full turn: 360°, which mathematicians also call 2π radians.

Why "230 V" isn't the peak

The voltage is only at its peak for an instant, and spends a lot of each cycle near zero. So the peak would overstate what the supply can actually do. Instead, AC is described by its RMS (root mean square) value: the DC voltage that would produce the same heating in a resistor.

For a sine wave, VRMS = Vpeak√2, so Vpeak = 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V. Every mains voltage you'll see quoted, including 230 V and 400 V, is an RMS value, and meters read RMS too. But insulation has to withstand the 325 V peaks, not just the 230 V average.

Converting between DC and AC

  • A rectifier converts AC into DC, using diodes, which only let current through one way. Every phone charger, LED driver and EV battery charger has one.
  • An inverter converts DC into AC. A solar PV system's inverter turns the panels' DC into 230 V, 50 Hz AC to use in the building or export to the grid. Battery storage works the same way.
  • A transformer changes an AC voltage up or down, but can't work on DC (see The grid and transformers).

Why DC needs extra care

  • Arcs are harder to break. When contacts open on load, an arc forms. On AC it usually goes out at the next zero crossing, within 10 ms. On DC there's no zero crossing, so the arc can keep burning. Switches, isolators and circuit breakers on DC circuits, such as a solar PV system's DC isolators, must be rated for DC at the voltage and current involved.
  • Solar panels can't be switched off. In daylight they produce voltage whenever light falls on them. The DC cabling between panels and inverter stays live even when the AC side is isolated.
  • DC leakage can stop an RCD working. Some equipment, such as car chargers and inverters, can leak smooth DC current in a fault. Smooth DC can stop the simpler RCD types from tripping (it "blinds" them).
  • Shock risk is different, not absent. At mains frequency, AC is generally more dangerous to the heart than the same level of DC, but DC can still kill. BS 7671 counts as extra-low voltage anything up to 50 V AC, or 120 V ripple-free DC.
