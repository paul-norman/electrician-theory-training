Electrical principles

Impedance and power factor

On AC, coils and capacitors oppose current in a different way from resistance. Together they make impedance, which also explains why a motor can draw more current than its wattage suggests.

Key takeaways

On AC, current is opposed by resistance (R), which turns energy into heat, and by reactance (X), which stores energy and hands it back each cycle. Coils (inductors) have inductive reactance XL = 2πfL, which rises with frequency. Capacitors have capacitive reactance XC = 12πfC, which falls with frequency. The two oppose each other. Together, resistance and reactance make impedance: Z = √(R² + X²), in ohms, and Ohm's law becomes I = VZ. Reactance shifts the current out of step with the voltage: it lags in an inductive circuit and leads in a capacitive one. Power factor = cos φ = RZ = PS: real power P in watts, apparent power S = V × I in volt-amperes (VA), and reactive power Q in var. Motors have a lagging power factor, which capacitors can correct.

Resistance isn't the whole story on AC

On DC, only resistance limits the current. On AC, the voltage and current keep changing, and two kinds of component react to that change:

  • An inductor is a coil of wire, as in a motor winding, a transformer or a fluorescent light's ballast. A changing current makes a changing magnetic field, which induces a voltage that opposes the change. The faster the current changes, the more it's opposed.
  • A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. It stores charge, and on AC it charges and discharges every half-cycle. The faster the changes, the more easily current flows "through" it.

This opposition is called reactance (X), measured in ohms like resistance. The difference is that reactance doesn't turn energy into heat. It stores energy in a magnetic or electric field for part of each cycle and returns it to the supply.

Inductive and capacitive reactance

XL = 2πfL   XC = 12πfC

Here f is the frequency in hertz, L is inductance in henries (H), and C is capacitance in farads (F); in practice you'll see millihenries (mH) and microfarads (µF). Inductive reactance goes up with frequency. Capacitive reactance goes down. On DC (zero frequency), an inductor is just a piece of wire and a capacitor blocks current completely.

In a circuit with both, they work against each other, so the net reactance is X = XL − XC.

Impedance

Impedance (Z) is the total opposition to AC: resistance and reactance combined. They can't simply be added, because they act a quarter of a cycle out of step. They add like the sides of a right-angled triangle instead:

Z = √(R² + X²)

Ohm's law still works with Z in place of R: V = I × Z, and I = VZ.

For most cables at 50 Hz the reactance is tiny, so impedance and resistance are almost the same. That's why earlier lessons could treat Z like R. But the word matters: the earth fault loop impedance Zs you'll test is called an impedance because the loop includes the supply transformer's windings (see the earth fault loop explorer).

Phase shift and power factor

In a pure resistance, current and voltage rise and fall together: they're in phase. Add reactance and the current shifts by an angle φ (phi). In an inductive circuit the current lags behind the voltage; in a capacitive circuit it leads. A memory aid is "CIVIL": in a Capacitor, I leads V; V leads I in an inductor (L).

Because of the shift, part of the current isn't doing useful work: it just carries energy back and forth. Three kinds of power describe this:

PowerSymbolUnitWhat it is
Real (true) powerPwatt (W)Turned into heat, light or movement. What the meter bills.
Apparent powerSvolt-ampere (VA)Simply V × I: what the cables and supply actually have to carry.
Reactive powerQvolt-ampere reactive (var)Stored and returned each cycle by inductors and capacitors.

They form a triangle of their own, so S² = P² + Q². The power factor is the share of the apparent power that does real work:

power
factor = cos φ = PS = RZ

A heater has a power factor of 1. A motor might be 0.8 lagging. Then P = V × I × cos φ: a motor with a power factor of 0.8, drawing 10 A at 230 V, uses 1,840 W, not 2,300 W, but the cable still has to carry the full 10 A.

Why it matters on site

  • Cable and device sizing: size by the current actually drawn, which for a low power factor load is more than its wattage suggests.
  • Power factor correction: capacitors fitted across inductive loads cancel some of the reactance, bringing the power factor closer to 1. Older fluorescent fittings had a correction capacitor built in. Large commercial sites can be charged extra for a poor power factor.
  • Testing: the earth fault loop impedance (Zs) and the supply's external impedance (Ze) are impedances, measured in ohms.
