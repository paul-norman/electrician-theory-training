Electrical principles

Ohm's law

One simple rule links voltage, current and resistance. You'll use it, or something built on it, almost every day in this trade.

Key takeaways

V = I × R. Double the voltage and the current doubles; double the resistance and it halves. Cover the unknown in the triangle to get the formula, and convert mA, kΩ and MΩ to plain amps and ohms before you calculate. Current doesn't only take the easiest path. It splits across every parallel path, in inverse proportion to each one's resistance, so a person in parallel with a CPC (the "earth wire") still gets a share.

The idea

For most conductors, the current is proportional to the voltage:

  • Double the pressure (voltage) and you get double the current.
  • Double the resistance and you get half the current.

Written as an equation:

V = I × R

Volts equal amps times ohms. Rearranged, that gives I = VR and R = VI. Know any two and you can work out the third.

Worked examples

A kettle element has a resistance of 17.6 Ω. What current does it draw on 230 V? I = VR = 23017.6 I ≈ 13.1 A
A heater draws 8.7 A from a 230 V supply. What's its resistance? R = VI = 2308.7 R ≈ 26.4 Ω
0.4 A flows through a 30 Ω resistor. What's the voltage across it? V = I × R = 0.4 × 30 V = 12 V

Watch the units

Ohm's law questions

Current takes every path

You'll often hear that "current takes the path of least resistance". That's only half true, and the false half is dangerous. When there are several paths, current flows through all of them. Each path gets a share that depends on its own resistance: I = VR for each path. The easy path gets the most, but the harder paths still get some. (Paths side by side like this are called parallel paths; see Series and parallel circuits.)
