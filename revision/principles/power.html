Power

Power tells you how fast a load turns electrical energy into heat, light or movement. It's the number on the side of every appliance, and it tells you what current the circuit has to carry.

Key takeaways

Power (P, in watts) is how fast energy is converted: P = V × I, or P = I²R and P = V²R. I = PV turns an appliance rating into the current its circuit must carry. A 3 kW kettle draws about 13 A and a 9.5 kW shower about 41 A, which is why a shower has its own circuit. Energy is power × time, and bills charge it in kWh. Heat in a cable rises with the square of the current: double the current, four times the heat.

Power is a rate

Power is how quickly energy is converted. One watt means one joule of energy every second. A 3,000 W (3 kW) kettle turns 3,000 joules of electrical energy into heat every second.

The symbol for power is P and the unit is the watt (W). A load is anything that uses that energy: a lamp, a kettle, a motor.

Power depends on both the pressure (voltage) and the flow (current):

P = V × I

Watts equal volts times amps. Rearranged: I = PV and V = PI. The one you'll use most is I = PV: it turns an appliance's power rating into the current its circuit has to carry.

The power triangle

Two more versions, from Ohm's law

Combine P = V × I with V = I × R and you get two more formulas. They're worth knowing because they show where heat comes from.

  • P = I² × R: replace V with I × R. Useful when you know the current through a resistance, such as a cable.
  • P = V²R: replace I with VR. Useful when you know the voltage across a load, such as a heater element.

The I² in the first one matters a lot. Double the current through a cable and you get four times the heat.

Quick estimates on site

At 230 V, 1 kW draws about 4.35 A (1,000230 ≈ 4.35). Many electricians round it to "about 4 A per kilowatt" for a rough first check, but that underestimates, so use the proper sum for anything that matters. The same formula works backwards: a circuit breaker's rating tells you how much load it can carry, P = V × I.

What current does a 10 kW cooker draw on 230 V (before any diversity)? I = PV = 10,000230 ≈ 43.5 A
How many 100 W lamps can a 6 A lighting circuit carry at 230 V? P = V × I = 230 × 6 = 1,380 W
1,380100 = 13.8 13 lamps (always round down)
Roughly what does a 3 kW immersion heater draw, using 4.35 A per kW? 3 × 4.35 ≈ 13 A

Round the number of lamps down: 14 lamps would need 1,400 W, which is more than the circuit can supply. Diversity, which allows for not everything running at once, is covered in Design current and diversity.

Power and energy aren't the same

Power is how fast energy is used. Energy is how much is used in total: power × time. Electricity bills charge for energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh). 1 kWh is a 1 kW load running for one hour.

A 2 kW heater runs for 3 hours. How much energy does it use? Energy = P × t = 2 kW × 3 h 6 kWh
A 3 kW kettle boils for 4 minutes. At 25p per kWh, what does it cost? 3 kW × 460 h = 0.2 kWh
0.2 kWh × 25p = 5p 5p
A 9.5 kW shower runs for 10 minutes. How much energy does it use? 9.5 kW × 1060 h ≈ 1.58 kWh

The current goes out along the line conductor and back along the neutral, so a 20 m run has 40 m of conductor. Doubling the current from 16 A to 32 A quadruples the heat. Thicker cable has less resistance, so it wastes less and runs cooler. That's what cable sizing is about, and it's the same reason the grid uses very high voltages (see The grid and transformers).
