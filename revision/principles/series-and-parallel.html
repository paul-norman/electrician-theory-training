Electrical principles

Series and parallel circuits

There are only two ways to connect loads together. Which one you choose decides how the voltage and current are shared, and whether one switch turns everything off.

Key takeaways

In series, loads sit one after another on a single path: the same current flows through all of them, resistances add (RT = R1 + R2 + R3), the supply voltage is shared between them, and one open switch or broken load stops everything. In parallel, each load has its own path across the full supply voltage: the branch currents add up to the total, each load can be switched on its own, and the total resistance is always lower than the smallest branch (1RT = 1R1 + 1R2 + 1R3). Kirchhoff's current law says the current into any junction equals the current out. Kirchhoff's voltage law says the voltage drops around any loop add up to the supply voltage. The loads in a house are all wired in parallel; a switch goes in series with the load it controls.

Switches

A switch is a deliberate break in a conductor. Closed (on), its contacts touch and current can flow. Open (off), there's an air gap, and since air is an insulator the loop is broken and no current flows anywhere in it.

A switch always goes in series with the load it controls, and in the line conductor, so that "off" disconnects the load from the live supply (see Your supply).

Series circuits

In a series circuit the loads are connected end to end, so there's only one path for the current.

  • The current is the same everywhere. Every bit of charge has to pass through every load.
  • Resistances add up: RT = R1 + R2 + R3. Each extra load makes the total bigger, so less current flows.
  • The supply voltage is shared out. Each load gets a share in proportion to its resistance, and the shares add up to the supply voltage. So no load gets the full voltage it was designed for.
  • One break stops everything. Open any switch, or let any load fail, and the whole loop goes dead. Old Christmas tree lights worked this way: one blown bulb and the whole string went out.

Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit each load is connected directly across the supply, on its own branch. That fixes both of the series problems:

  • Every load gets the full supply voltage, so each runs as designed.
  • Each branch can be switched on its own. Turning one load off doesn't affect the others.
  • Branch currents add up. The supply provides the total: IT = I1 + I2 + I3.
  • The total resistance falls as you add branches, because each branch is another path for current. It's always lower than the smallest single branch:

1RT = 1R1 + 1R2 + 1R3

Work out the right-hand side, then turn the answer upside down to get RT. For just two resistors there's a shortcut, "product over sum": RT = R1 × R2R1 + R2. And for identical resistors, divide one by how many there are: four 100 Ω in parallel make 25 Ω.

Every socket, light and appliance in a building is wired in parallel, which is why each one gets 230 V and can be switched independently.

Kirchhoff's laws

Two rules, both from the 1840s, hold in every circuit, however complicated. They're what make the series and parallel rules above work.

Kirchhoff's current law (the first law): at any junction, the current flowing in equals the current flowing out. Put another way, the currents at a junction add up to zero if you count "in" as positive and "out" as negative. Charge can't pile up at a point or vanish, so whatever arrives has to leave. That's why the branch currents in a parallel circuit add up to the supply current, and why the current is the same all the way round a series loop.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (the second law): go round any closed loop and the voltage drops across the loads and cables add up to the supply voltage. Counting the supply as a rise and each drop as a fall, the total round the loop is zero. That's why series loads share the supply voltage, and why, by the time the current reaches the neutral on its way back, almost all of the 230 V has been used. (The neutral isn't exactly 0 V: a few volts are dropped along it too. See Your supply.)

What will the meter read?

Kirchhoff's voltage law lets you predict any voltmeter reading, which is exactly what you'll do when testing and fault-finding. Three rules cover it:

  • Two points on the same piece of conductor read 0 V. A good conductor drops almost no voltage, so everything joined by plain wire (or a closed switch) is at the same potential.
  • A load only drops voltage while current flows through it. With current, the lamp drops the supply voltage. With no current, both its ends sit at the same potential, so it reads 0 V.
  • An open switch has the full supply voltage across its gap. The voltage has to be dropped somewhere round the loop, and with no current the gap is the only place left. That's why the terminal of a switched-off circuit can still be live.
