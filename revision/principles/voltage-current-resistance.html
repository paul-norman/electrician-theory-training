Electrical principles

Voltage, current and resistance

Three quantities describe every circuit you'll ever work on. Get these straight and the rest of the course gets much easier.

Key takeaways

Voltage (V or U, in volts) is the electrical pressure between two points. Current (I, in amps) is how much charge flows past a point each second. Resistance (R, in ohms) opposes the flow and turns energy into heat, and it rises with longer, thinner or hotter conductors. Measure voltage across a component and current in series with it. In a series circuit the current is the same everywhere, and the voltages across the loads add up to the supply. Circuit diagrams use conventional current, which flows from + to −.

A water comparison helps at first. Voltage is like the pressure difference between two ends of a pipe. Current is the flow rate. Resistance is how narrow the pipe is. More pressure pushes more flow; a narrower pipe lets less through. Don't push it too far, though: electricity doesn't leak out of an open switch the way water leaks out of a cut pipe.

Voltage: the pressure

Voltage is properly called potential difference (p.d.). The key word is difference: voltage always exists between two points. "The voltage at this wire" really means "between this wire and some reference", usually earth or neutral. That's why a voltmeter has two leads.

Charge is measured in coulombs (C). One coulomb is the charge carried by about 6.24 × 1018 electrons, which is the amount that flows past a point each second when the current is 1 A. One volt means each coulomb of charge gets (or gives up) one joule of energy. The voltage a source produces, like a battery or transformer, is called its electromotive force (e.m.f.). The voltage that appears across a component is a potential difference.

BS 7671 uses U for voltage. You'll see U₀ = 230 V a lot: the nominal voltage between line and earth in the UK.

Current: the flow

Current is the rate that charge passes a point. One amp means one coulomb per second, which is about 6,240,000,000,000,000,000 electrons per second. The symbol I comes from the French intensité.

To measure current, the meter must be in series: the current has to flow through it. On site you'll normally use a clamp meter instead, which senses the magnetic field around a single conductor without breaking the circuit.

Which way does it flow? Electrons are negative, so they flow from − to +. But long before electrons were discovered, scientists had already agreed that current flows from + to −. That convention stuck. It's called conventional current and it's what every circuit diagram, rule and textbook in the trade uses.

Resistance: the opposition

Resistance is how hard it is for current to flow. As electrons bump through the lattice they give up energy as heat: useful in a kettle element, wasted in a cable. A conductor's resistance goes up when it is:

  • longer: twice the length, twice the resistance
  • thinner: half the cross-section (mm²), twice the resistance
  • hotter: see the lattice animation
  • made of a poorer conductor: aluminium has about 1.6 × the resistance of copper

You'll also see Z, for impedance. It's the AC version of resistance, which also includes effects from coils and capacitors. It's still measured in ohms. For most circuit calculations in this course you can treat Z like R.

Circuit symbols

Circuit diagrams use a standard set of symbols, so anyone can read them. These are the UK and international ones (from the standard BS EN 60617), and they're the symbols used in every diagram in these notes. Older or American material may draw a resistor as a zigzag; it means the same thing.

Things to try: double the supply voltage and watch the current double. Check the three orange boxes: the current is identical before the lamp, between the loads and on the way back. Raise the lamp's resistance and watch it take a bigger share of the voltage. Turn the heater off and the lamp gets the whole supply. And the voltages across the loads always add up to the supply voltage.

Voltage gets shared out

Follow the loop from the + terminal. The voltage drops a little across each load, and by the time you're back at the − terminal it has all been used. Loads in series share the supply voltage in proportion to their resistance: the bigger resistance takes the bigger share. This rule is called Kirchhoff's voltage law (more in Series and parallel circuits).

On a real 230 V circuit, the cable has a tiny resistance too, so a few volts are lost along the cable before the load gets its share. That's voltage drop, and BS 7671 limits how much you're allowed. You'll calculate it later in the course.
