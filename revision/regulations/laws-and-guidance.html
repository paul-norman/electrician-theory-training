Regulations and compliance

Laws and guidance

Which rules are law, which are guidance, and where the Building Regulations fit. Knowing the difference tells you what you must do, and how you show you've done it.

Key takeaways

Statutory documents are law: Acts of Parliament (such as the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974) and the Regulations made under them (such as the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 and the Building Regulations). Breaking them is a criminal offence. Non-statutory documents are guidance: the Approved Documents, BS 7671, the On-Site Guide and the Guidance Notes. They aren't law themselves, but following them is the normal way to show you've met the law. The Building Regulations are split into Parts A to T, each explained by an Approved Document; Part P covers electrical safety in dwellings in England and Wales. Part M puts switches and sockets in new homes between 450 mm and 1,200 mm above the finished floor.

Statutory and non-statutory

Statutory means "made by law". An Act of Parliament sets out broad duties, and Regulations (statutory instruments) made under it add the detail. These are legally binding: if you break them you can be prosecuted, fined or, in serious cases, imprisoned.

Non-statutory documents aren't law. They're the guidance and standards that explain how to meet the law. Nobody can prosecute you just for not following BS 7671. But if something goes wrong, following it is the strongest evidence that you did the job properly, and departing from it means you'd have to prove your way was just as safe.

The laws you'll work under

  • Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 (often HASAWA). The main health and safety law. Employers must keep employees and anyone affected by their work safe, as far as reasonably practicable. Employees must take reasonable care of themselves and others, and co-operate with their employer on safety.
  • Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 (EAWR). Made under that Act, specifically for electricity. Systems must be built and maintained to prevent danger. There must be a way to cut off the supply and isolate equipment. Work should be done dead: live working is only allowed when it would be unreasonable for the equipment to be dead, it's reasonable to work live, and suitable precautions are taken. Anyone doing the work must be competent, or properly supervised.
  • Building Regulations 2010. Set minimum standards for building work, including electrical work in homes. England and Wales each have their own version; Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate systems altogether.
  • Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations 2002 (ESQCR). Mostly the DNO's duties, such as keeping the supply within its limits, and why you mustn't connect anything to their network without permission.

BS 7671 sits underneath all of these. It isn't law, but the official guidance for both the Electricity at Work Regulations and Part P points to it. So in practice, an installation that meets BS 7671 is very likely to meet the law too.

The Building Regulations and Approved Documents

The Building Regulations are split into Parts, each a short legal requirement (for example, Part P says electrical installations must be designed and installed to protect people from fire and injury). Each Part has an Approved Document explaining ways to meet it. The ones an electrician runs into most:

The full set in England runs from A to T. There's no I, and N was folded into K. The others are D (toxic substances), G (sanitation, hot water safety and water efficiency), H (drainage and waste disposal), J (combustion appliances and fuel storage), K (protection from falling, collision and impact), O (overheating), Q (security) and T (toilet accommodation, added in 2024 for new non-domestic buildings). Wales has its own set, which differs in places.

Part M: switch and socket heights

Measured to the centre of the accessory, from the finished floor (the floor surface once it's laid, not the bare concrete). The band keeps switches and sockets within easy reach for wheelchair users and people who can't bend easily. It applies to new homes; when you're working in an existing one, matching the existing heights is usually acceptable.

The band is for switches and sockets that people use day to day. Outlets that aren't meant to be reached regularly can go where the equipment needs them: behind a wall-mounted TV, above a kitchen worktop, behind a fridge, or up by an extractor fan or cooker hood.
