Regulations and compliance

Part P and notification

Where Part P applies, which jobs have to be reported to building control, the three ways to get them signed off, and the certificates that go with them.

Key takeaways

Part P of the Building Regulations covers electrical safety in dwellings in England and Wales. It also covers outbuildings and businesses that share a dwelling's supply, and the common areas of blocks of flats (but not lifts). All electrical work in a dwelling must be safe and meet the requirements, but only notifiable work has to be reported to building control. In England that's a new circuit, a replacement consumer unit, or an addition or alteration to a circuit in a special location. In a bathroom, that means only the space within 0.6 m of the bath or shower tray, up to 2.25 m high, not the whole room. Wales adds more: the whole bathroom, kitchens, outdoor work, and some systems such as solar panels and underfloor heating. Notifiable work is signed off one of three ways: by a CPS member (such as NICEIC or NAPIT) self-certifying, by notifying building control before starting (a building notice at least two working days ahead), or through a registered third-party certifier. Every job also gets a BS 7671 certificate: an EIC for new circuits and consumer units, a MEIWC for minor works, and an EICR to report on an existing installation.

Where Part P applies

Part P applies to electrical installations in and around dwellings (houses and flats) in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own building regulations. Part P also reaches some places that aren't homes:

  • Outbuildings and gardens supplied from a dwelling: sheds, garages, greenhouses, garden lighting.
  • Business premises that share a supply with a dwelling, such as a shop with a flat above it on the same supply. With its own separate supply, the shop isn't covered.
  • Common parts of blocks of flats: corridors, stairs and shared facilities such as a residents' gym or laundry. Lifts are excluded.

A block of flats is often fed by a three-phase supply that a landlord's distribution board splits into single-phase supplies, one per flat. Each flat then has its own consumer unit.

Notifiable and non-notifiable work

Every job in a dwelling must meet Part P and BS 7671, whether it's notifiable or not. "Notifiable" only means building control must be told so it can be checked.

In England, notifiable work is:

  • installing a new circuit
  • replacing a consumer unit
  • any addition or alteration to an existing circuit in a special location

For notification in England, a special location is one of these:

  • a room containing a swimming pool or a sauna heater (the whole room)
  • in a room with a bath or shower, the space around the bath or shower, not the whole room. It runs from the finished floor up to 2.25 m, or up to the fixed shower head if that's higher. It reaches 0.6 m out from the edge of the bath or shower tray. With no tray, it reaches 1.2 m from the centre of the fixed shower head.

That space is roughly zones 1 and 2 from Bathrooms and special locations, but the definition comes from the Building Regulations, not BS 7671. So in England, a light point added to an existing circuit 0.4 m from the bath is notifiable, but one added on the far side of the bathroom isn't. Both still have to meet the bathroom rules in BS 7671 Section 701, such as 30 mA RCD protection.

Everything else is non-notifiable (often called minor works). That includes adding a socket or light point to an existing circuit outside a special location, replacing switches and sockets, and replacing a damaged cable like for like.

Wales: stricter rules

England changed its rules in 2013. Wales kept the older, wider list, so more jobs are notifiable there. As well as new circuits and consumer unit replacements, Wales treats these as places or installations where any addition or alteration is notifiable:

  • a room containing a bath or shower (the whole room, not just the space around the bath)
  • swimming and paddling pools, and hot air saunas
  • kitchens (see below)
  • electric floor or ceiling heating
  • garden lighting or power, including outdoor sockets (solar-powered garden lights don't count)
  • solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, and small generators such as micro combined heat and power (CHP) units
  • extra-low-voltage lighting, unless it's a pre-assembled, CE-marked lighting set

In Wales a kitchen is a room, or part of a room, with a sink and somewhere to prepare food. In an open-plan room, the kitchen counts as reaching 3 m from the edge of the sink, or to a nearer dividing wall. So a socket added 2 m from the sink in a kitchen-diner in Wales is notifiable, but one added 4 m away is minor work.

Replacing accessories such as sockets and switches, and like-for-like repairs, aren't notifiable in either country. For any job in Wales, check the current Welsh Approved Document P.

Three ways to get notifiable work signed off

Route 1Competent Person Scheme (CPS)If you're registered with a scheme such as NICEIC or NAPIT, you can self-certify. You do the work, certify it, and the scheme notifies building control for you, normally within 30 days of finishing. The customer then receives a Building Regulations compliance certificate. Most domestic electricians work this way.
Route 2Building controlTell the local authority's building control before the work starts: usually with a building notice, given at least two working days in advance (full plans for bigger projects). They inspect, and charge a fee. When they're satisfied, they issue a completion certificate.
Route 3Registered third-party certifierAn electrician who isn't in a scheme can use a registered third-party certifier. The certifier inspects the work at set stages, not just at the end, often needing photos before things are covered up. They then certify it and notify building control.

Certificates for the customer

Separately from Building Regulations sign-off, BS 7671 requires paperwork for the work itself:

DocumentUsed for
Electrical Installation Certificate (EIC)New installations, new circuits and consumer unit replacements. Records the design, construction, inspection and test results.
Minor Electrical Installation Works Certificate (MEIWC)Additions or alterations that don't include a new circuit, such as an extra socket on an existing circuit.
Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR)Reporting on the condition of an existing installation. It doesn't certify new work.

An EICR is often a sensible first step before taking on work in an older property. BS 7671 says that before adding to or altering an installation, you must check that the existing parts you rely on, such as the earthing and the consumer unit, are adequate for it. An EICR codes each problem it finds: C1 danger present, C2 potentially dangerous, C3 improvement recommended, and FI further investigation needed.

Getting it wrong

Not notifying notifiable work breaks the Building Regulations. The local authority can prosecute (fines can be large), and can require the work to be opened up, corrected or removed. The customer is left without the paperwork they'll need when selling the house, and insurers may be reluctant to pay out after a fire.
