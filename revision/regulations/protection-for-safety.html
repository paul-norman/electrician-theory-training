Part 4: protection for safety

A tour of the Part of BS 7671 that carries the most exam questions: what each chapter and section protects against, so you know where to turn.

Key takeaways

Part 4 has a chapter per danger: 41 electric shock, 42 thermal effects (fire and burns), 43 overcurrent, 44 voltage and electromagnetic disturbances, 46 isolation and switching (there's no 45). Chapter 41's sections are the protective measures: 411 automatic disconnection (with TN, TT, IT, FELV and RLV inside it), 412 double or reinforced insulation, 413 electrical separation, 414 SELV and PELV, 415 additional protection (30 mA RCDs and supplementary bonding), 416 basic protection, 417 obstacles and placing out of reach, 418 measures for skilled supervision only, 419 when automatic disconnection isn't feasible. Chapter 43 holds Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz and I2 ≤ 1.45 × Iz, and the equation for how long a cable survives a fault: t = k2 S2I2. The maximum RCD rating for fire protection is 300 mA.

Chapter 41: electric shock

The sections of Chapter 41 are the protective measures you can choose from. Most circuits use only 411, but the rest exist for particular situations (see BS 7671 and its definitions for basic, fault and additional protection).

Inside Section 411

Section 411 is twelve pages long because it covers automatic disconnection on every kind of system. Learn the order and you can find anything in it:

  • 411.3: the general requirements: protective earthing, protective bonding, the disconnection times of Table 41.1, and additional protection for sockets (411.3.3) and home lighting (411.3.4).
  • 411.4: TN systems, with the maximum Zs Tables 41.2 to 41.4.
  • 411.5: TT systems, where an RCD normally does the disconnecting and RA × IΔn must not exceed 50 V. A 30 mA RCD allows up to about 1,667 Ω, which is why RCDs rescue circuits whose Zs is too high for a breaker.
  • 411.6: IT systems. The supply is isolated from earth, or earthed through a high impedance, so a first fault to earth draws hardly any current and nothing needs to trip. An insulation monitoring device (IMD) or residual current monitor (RCM) raises an alarm instead, and maintenance staff find and fix the fault before a second one happens. That suits places where a sudden power cut is itself dangerous, such as operating theatres. (I stands for isolated or impedance; T for the installation's own earth, from the Latin terra.)
  • 411.7: functional extra-low voltage (FELV): extra-low voltage used because the equipment needs it, not as a safety measure, where the source or wiring doesn't meet all the SELV or PELV rules. Its protection comes from the measures on the supply side of the transformer.
  • 411.8: reduced low voltage (RLV): the 110 V building-site system. The yellow transformer's output winding is centre-tapped to earth, so each line is only 55 V to earth (63.5 V on three-phase), while tools still get 110 V. It has its own maximum Zs table.

Basic protection (Section 416)

  • Insulation must cover live parts completely and be removable only by destroying it. Stripping a cable destroys the insulation you take off.
  • Barriers and enclosures must be at least IP2X or IPXXB: a finger (12.5 mm) can't get in. The top of an enclosure that's easy to reach must be at least IP4X or IPXXD: nothing over 1 mm gets in. Things dropped on top, and dust, are the worry.
  • You must only be able to open an enclosure or remove a barrier with a key or tool, or after the supply has been disconnected, or if an inner barrier of at least IPXXB, itself needing a tool to remove, stays in place.

See the IP code decoder for what the digits mean.

Obstacles and placing out of reach (Section 417)

Bare live parts are allowed if an obstacle stops you touching them by accident, or they're beyond arm's reach (2.5 m up). Think of crane rails, the live ceiling grid of fairground dodgems, or a conductor rail feeding a robot along a factory line. Power lines and the Underground's live rail work the same way, though they're outside BS 7671. These measures are only for areas controlled or supervised by skilled or instructed people, never a home.

Section 418 goes further: a non-conducting location has no earthed metalwork at all and insulating floors and walls, so there's nothing at a different potential to touch. Again, skilled supervision only.

Chapter 42: thermal effects

Any connection can work loose, overheat and start a fire, even in a light switch, so this chapter applies to everything you install.

  • Section 421, fire caused by equipment. A consumer unit in a home must have a non-combustible enclosure, usually steel, or sit in a non-combustible cabinet (421.1.201; see Consumer units and protective devices). Existing plastic boards can stay. Arc fault detection devices (AFDDs) (421.1.7) are required on final circuits with socket-outlets up to 32 A in higher-risk residential buildings (HRRBs: at least 18 m or seven storeys high), houses in multiple occupation (HMOs), purpose-built student accommodation and care homes, and recommended elsewhere. AFDDs cost far more than a breaker, so on a block of flats they can add thousands to a quote. Check the current wording in your copy: this rule may well widen.
  • Section 422, locations with particular fire risks. Escape routes should only carry wiring that's needed there, such as fire safety equipment and the lighting, kept as short as possible. Where combustible materials are processed or stored (a barn, a woodwork shop), luminaires must have limited surface temperatures and the wiring needs RCD protection of no more than 300 mA, the figure for fire protection. Separate rules cover buildings made of combustible materials such as timber frames, fire-propagating structures (shaped so fire spreads, like a chimney effect), and buildings of national, commercial, industrial or public significance, such as historic monuments.
  • Section 423, burns. Table 42.1 limits the surface temperature of parts you can touch.
  • Section 424, overheating of forced-air heating and hot water equipment.

Chapter 43: overcurrent

Chapter 43 deals with overloads and short circuits between live conductors. Earth faults belong to Chapter 41. Things you'll find here:

  • Never switch the neutral on its own. If a device breaks the neutral, it must break the line conductors at the same time, or the equipment stays live with the neutral open.
  • Section 433, overload. The design rule Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz, and I2 ≤ 1.45 × Iz (see Design current and diversity). Ring final circuits are here too.
  • 433.2, where the device goes. Normally where the cable's current-carrying capacity drops, such as a change to a smaller cable. An extension lead is a familiar example: a 1.25 mm² flex plugged into a 32 A ring is fine because the 13 A fuse in the plug protects it.
  • 433.3, leaving the overload device out. Sometimes allowed, and sometimes recommended where tripping on overload would itself be dangerous, such as a fire pump or a lifting magnet.
  • Section 434, fault current. The device must clear a short circuit before the conductors overheat. That's checked with the equation below.

How long will a cable survive a fault?

During a short circuit, the current is so big that the conductor heats up faster than it can lose heat. Regulation 434.5.2 gives the time it can survive before its insulation is damaged:

t = k2 × S2I2

where t is in seconds, S is the conductor's cross-sectional area in mm², I is the fault current in amps, and k is a factor from Table 43.1 that depends on the conductor and insulation. For copper with 70 °C thermoplastic (PVC) insulation, like twin and earth, k = 115: it can run at 70 °C and is damaged at 160 °C. If the cable survives longer than the device takes to disconnect, the circuit passes. Exam questions usually ask you to look up k rather than do the whole sum.

Chapter 44: voltage and electromagnetic disturbances

Chapters 41 to 43 are about too much current. Chapter 44 is about voltages you didn't expect:

  • Section 443, transient overvoltages from lightning or switching on the network. This is where the rules for surge protective devices (SPDs) are.
  • Section 444, electromagnetic disturbances: interference between circuits and equipment.
  • Section 445, undervoltage. If the power fails and comes back, a machine such as a lathe mustn't restart by itself while someone is leaning over it. An undervoltage release switches it off when the voltage drops, so it has to be restarted deliberately.

Chapter 46: isolation and switching

Which switching device to use, and where one is needed. There are four purposes: isolation (cutting off the supply so you can work safely), switching off for mechanical maintenance, emergency switching (cutting off quickly to remove a danger) and functional switching (normal control, like a light switch). Chapter 53 covers choosing and fitting the devices themselves.

Using the book on a real job

The Parts are in the order you'd use them. Say a customer asks you to fit an EV charger:

  1. Part 1: is it in scope? Yes, EV charging is covered.
  2. Chapter 13: the fundamental principles it must meet: shock, thermal effects, overcurrent, fault current, voltage disturbances.
  3. Part 2: use the defined terms in your quote and your order to the wholesaler, so nobody can misread them.
  4. Part 3: the supply: phases, main fuse, earthing arrangement, and whether the existing installation can take the load.
  5. Part 4: which protective measures: almost certainly 411, perhaps 412 if the charger is Class II.
  6. Parts 5 and 6: choosing and installing the cable and devices (see Part 5: selection and erection), then inspecting and testing.
  7. Part 7: Section 722 changes some of the general rules for EV charging.

Making up a job like this and listing the sections you'd use is a good way to revise.
