Part 5: selection and erection

Choosing the equipment and putting it in: the second-biggest share of exam questions, and the Part you'll use most on site.

Key takeaways

Part 5 follows a job: Chapter 51 common rules (suits the voltage, current and frequency; external influences; accessibility; identification and notices), 52 wiring systems (Tables 4A1 and 4A2, external influences in 522, current-carrying capacity in 523, voltage drop in 525, connections in 526, fire spread in 527, proximity in 528), 53 protection, isolation, switching, control and monitoring (RCD types, AFDDs, SPDs, selectivity, switches), 54 earthing and protective conductors, and 55 other equipment. Green and yellow is only ever a protective conductor, with each colour covering 30% to 70%; functional earthing conductors are pink. A warning notice is needed where the voltage to earth exceeds 230 V and wouldn't be expected, so a normal 400 V three-phase board doesn't need one under that rule. Leakage downstream of an RCD should be no more than 30% of its rating, 9 mA for a 30 mA RCD. A firefighter's switch is red and no more than 2.75 m up. A CPC is either selected from Table 54.7 (same size as the line up to 16 mm², 16 mm² up to 35 mm², half above that) or calculated with S = √(I2t)k, taking k from Tables 54.2 to 54.6. Motors over 0.37 kW need overload protection. Chapter 56 covers safety services and Chapter 57, new in Amendment 4, stationary batteries.

Chapter 51: common rules

Operating conditions (512.1)

Every item of equipment must suit the voltage, the design current and the frequency (50 Hz in the UK; see Your supply). U0 means the nominal voltage to earth (230 V) and U the voltage between lines (400 V); Part 2's list of symbols has them. Conductors that run hotter than 70 °C, such as 90 °C thermosetting cables, may only be connected to accessories and switchgear the manufacturer says can take that temperature. Equipment must also be compatible: it mustn't harm other equipment or the supply. Install it to the manufacturer's instructions as well as BS 7671.

External influences (512.2 and Appendix 5)

An external influence is anything outside the wiring system that could affect it: water, dust, heat, impact, sunlight, animals, even the people using it. It isn't only "outdoors": a consumer unit with a missing blank is open to dust and fingers. Appendix 5 gives each influence a code. The first letter is the category (A environment, B use, C building), the second the influence, and the number its severity: AD is water, AE solid objects, BA the capability of persons (BA1 ordinary to BA5 skilled). Regulation 522 has the matching installation rules.

Accessibility (513)

Equipment must be placed so you can operate, inspect, test, maintain and repair it, and get at its connections. A fused spur hidden behind pipes in an airing cupboard fails this. (Access for disabled users is a Building Regulations matter, Approved Document M, not this regulation.)

Green and yellow is used only for protective conductors. In the combination, each colour must cover at least 30% and at most 70% of the surface (514.4.2). Amendment 4 extended Table 51 with combined protective and functional conductors and added symbols, so check the table in your copy.

You can also identify conductors with letters or numbers (514.5), for example ferrules marked L1, L2, L3 on three single cores that are all brown. Sleeving does the same job: a blue core used as a switch line gets brown sleeving. Underline a 6 or 9 so it can't be read upside down. Where old colours meet new, mark both and fit a caution notice (see Installing cables): old three-phase was red, yellow, blue with a black neutral, so blue and black have changed jobs.

Diagrams and notices (514.9 to 514.16)

  • Diagrams and charts showing each circuit, what it supplies and its protective device. In a home, the certificate and schedules from Appendix 6 can do this job. In bigger installations, a framed schedule next to the board is common practice.
  • Voltage warning (514.10): needed where a nominal voltage exceeding 230 V to earth exists and wouldn't be expected. On a normal three-phase board, each line is still 230 V to earth, so this rule doesn't require a "400 V" label, though you may fit one. Read exam questions on this carefully.
  • Periodic inspection and testing notice giving the date of the next inspection.
  • RCD test notice asking users to press the test button every six months. The tutor said it's no longer required in homes; check the current wording in your copy.
  • Alternative or multiple supplies, such as solar PV or a generator: a warning at the origin, the meter and the board, so nobody thinks the installation is dead when they've switched off only one source.

Mutual detrimental influence (515)

The installation mustn't harm other services, and they mustn't harm it: a cable run against a hot pipe, or a power cable alongside a data cable, whose magnetic field can cause interference.

Chapter 52: wiring systems

Which cable can go where (Tables 4A1 and 4A2)

Appendix 4 has two tables to start with. Table 4A2 lists the installation reference methods (A, B, C, 100 and so on, with pictures), used for cable sizing and recorded on certificates (see Cable sizing). Table 4A1 says which methods each cable type may use. In short:

A non-sheathed cable has insulation (basic protection) but no sheath to protect it mechanically, so it must be enclosed. The sheath lets a cable be clipped direct or laid on tray, though you might still choose conduit or trunking where it could be damaged. Table 4A1 has more columns (insulators, support wires, without fixings) and separate rows for single-core and multicore, so use it for exact answers.

General rules (521)

  • Every conductor of a circuit through the same hole in a steel enclosure (521.5.1). Line and neutral in separate holes leave each surrounded by its own magnetic field, which induces eddy currents in the steel and heats it.
  • No borrowed neutrals: each circuit's line and neutral must stay together (see Lighting circuits). Isolating one circuit can leave a "dead" neutral carrying current from the other. With RCDs, both circuits' RCDs see an imbalance and trip.
  • Flexible cables may be used for fixed wiring only if they're heavy duty, unless the risk of damage is low or they're protected (521.9). A flex is normal from an isolator to an appliance, such as heat-resistant flex to an immersion heater.
  • Premature collapse (521.10.202): wiring must be supported so it can't drop in a fire and trap firefighters. Use metal clips, saddles or fixings; galvanised strapping ("builder's band") inside trunking works well. See Installing cables.

External influences (522)

One regulation group per influence: 522.2 heat, 522.3 water, 522.4 solid objects, 522.6 impact (including the safe zones and the new Table 52.1 for cables in walls and partitions in Amendment 4), and 522.8.10 cables buried in the ground (earthed armour or metal sheath, or a duct giving equal protection; marked with cable covers or tape; deep enough not to be disturbed, but no depth is given). All are covered in Installing cables.

Current, voltage drop and connections (523 to 526)

  • 523, current-carrying capacity: Table 52.2 gives the maximum operating temperature of each insulation (70 °C thermoplastic, 90 °C thermosetting); Appendix 4 gives the ratings and correction factors. Where conditions change along a route, size for the worst part, and only combine factors that apply at the same place. See Cable sizing.
  • 525, voltage drop: the voltage at the equipment must be enough for it to work properly. Appendix 4 gives the usual limits (3% for lighting, 5% for other circuits).
  • Bend radii aren't in BS 7671; the On-Site Guide has them.
  • 526, connections must give durable electrical continuity and enough mechanical strength. Screw terminals loosen over time, so they must stay accessible. Joints that may be inaccessible include those marked MF (maintenance-free), crimped, soldered, welded or compound-filled joints, and joints in the ground. Connector blocks must be inside an enclosure, never just taped up and pushed into a ceiling. Fine-stranded conductors need care so strands don't spread or break (526.9).

Fire spread and proximity (527 and 528)

  • 527: where wiring passes through a fire-resisting floor, wall or ceiling, seal the hole to its original fire resistance. Cutting downlights into a ceiling counts: use fire-rated fittings where the ceiling is a fire barrier.
  • 528: keep band I circuits (data, telecoms, ELV) separate from band II circuits (230 V), for example in separate compartments of dado trunking; and keep wiring clear of non-electrical services such as hot pipes.

Chapter 53: protection, isolation, switching, control and monitoring

  • 530.4, fixing: equipment fixed securely with fixings suited to the wall, and loose items (such as a DIN-rail bell transformer) inside an enclosure.
  • 531, devices for automatic disconnection: chosen to suit Section 411 and the earthing system. RCDs must break all live conductors (line and neutral). The CPC isn't a live conductor; it carries current only during a fault.
  • 531.3.2, unwanted tripping: computers and similar equipment leak a little current to earth in normal use. Add up the leakage downstream of an RCD and keep it to no more than 30% of the RCD's rated residual current. Try the calculator below.
  • 531.3.3, RCD types, shown by a symbol on the device: AC (pure AC only), A (also pulsating DC; the normal choice now, because LED drivers, computers and chargers can produce DC that blinds a Type AC), F (adds mixed frequencies, for inverter appliances) and B (adds smooth DC). RCD types are a different thing from the B, C and D curve types of circuit breakers.
  • 532, fire: an RCD used for fire protection is rated at no more than 300 mA. AFDDs (532.6) go at the origin of the circuit they protect, on single-phase AC circuits.
  • 533: the standards for fuses, circuit breakers and RCBOs used for overcurrent protection.
  • 534, surge protective devices: Types 1, 2 and 3 by where they sit (Type 1 at the origin where lightning can strike the building directly, Type 2 at boards, Type 3 next to sensitive equipment), and lightning protection zones. Follow the manufacturer for the SPD's own overcurrent protection.
  • 536, coordination and selectivity: the device nearest the fault should operate first. A 13 A plug fuse on a 20 A radial is the everyday example. 536.4.5 also requires a consumer unit (an assembly to BS EN 61439) to suit the fault current it could see, such as a 16 kA conditional rating.
  • 537, isolation and switching: four purposes (isolation, mechanical maintenance, emergency switching, functional switching). Table 537.4 lists device types and which purposes each can serve. In a switch symbol, a small circle means it can be operated on load.
  • Firefighter's switch (537.4): for high-voltage signs and lighting, such as neon signs on shopfronts and some outdoor lighting, so firefighters can kill it before using water. Red, clearly marked, in a conspicuous place reachable by firefighters, and no more than 2.75 m above the ground.
  • 538, monitoring: insulation monitoring devices (IMDs) for IT systems, and residual current monitors (RCMs).

Chapter 54: earthing and protective conductors

  • 542.2, earth electrodes: on a TT system the electrode is the installer's job, not the network operator's. 542.2.2 lists what can be used (rods or pipes, tapes or wires, plates, and some others), and Table 54.1 gives the minimum size of a buried earthing conductor.
  • 542.4, the main earthing terminal (MET): every installation has one. It can be the earth bar in the consumer unit or a separate terminal block. The earthing conductor, CPCs, main bonding, any functional earthing and any lightning protection all connect to it.
  • 543.1, CPC size: either calculated with the adiabatic equation (543.1.3) or selected from Table 54.7 (543.1.4). Table 54.7: a line conductor up to 16 mm² needs a protective conductor the same size; over 16 mm² up to 35 mm², 16 mm²; over 35 mm², half the line size, rounded up to the next size made (a 95 mm² line needs 50 mm², because 47.5 mm² isn't made). The table's other column is for a protective conductor made of a different material, such as SWA armour. That's why tails of 16 mm² get a 16 mm² earthing conductor, and singles circuits usually have a CPC the same size as the line. Twin and earth uses a smaller CPC (except 1 mm²), so it has to be checked by calculation.
  • 543.2, what can be a protective conductor: a core in a cable, a separate conductor, metal conduit or trunking, a cable's armour or sheath (SWA, MICC) and certain extraneous parts, if they meet the conditions. Separate protective conductors under 10 mm² must be copper.
  • 543.3, keeping it continuous: joints must be accessible, including BS 951 bonding clamps. A kitchen fitter who boxes in the stopcock and its bonding clamp, leaving only the handle poking out, breaks this rule. No switching device may be put in a protective conductor, with limited exceptions such as the open-PEN protection in some EV chargers on TN-C-S, which disconnects the CPC along with line and neutral if the supply neutral breaks.
  • 543.7, high protective conductor current: equipment leaking more than 10 mA to earth must be permanently connected, or use an industrial plug and socket (BS EN 60309-2) with a protective conductor of at least 2.5 mm² for 16 A plugs or 4 mm² above 16 A.
  • 544, bonding: main bonding sizes come from 544.1.1 or Table 54.8 for PME, connected within 600 mm of where the service enters, inside the building if the meter is outside. Supplementary bonding is 544.2. See Protective bonding.
  • 545: functional earthing and bonding for IT and communications equipment, including the main functional earthing terminal (MFET).

Is the CPC big enough?

The adiabatic equation (543.1.3) gives the smallest CPC that can carry the fault current until the device disconnects:

S = √(I2 × t)k

I is the fault current, from a measurement or calculation. t is the device's disconnection time at that current, read from its curve in Appendix 3. k comes from Tables 54.2 to 54.6, chosen by the type of protective conductor: 54.2 for a separate insulated conductor, 54.3 for a core in a cable (twin and earth), 54.4 for armour, 54.5 for steel conduit or trunking, 54.6 for a bare conductor. If the answer is no bigger than the CPC you have, it's big enough. Try the tutor's example below: a 32 A Type B ring in 2.5 mm² twin and earth with a 1.5 mm² CPC.

Chapter 55: other equipment

  • 551, generators: where a generator is an alternative to the mains, a changeover switch must stop the two running in parallel. Where it does run in parallel (551.7), it connects on the supply side of the final circuits' protection, and the switchgear must be rated for both sources. Chapter 57 counts stationary batteries as generating sets.
  • 552, motors: motors over 0.37 kW need overload protection, and a motor shouldn't restart by itself after a supply interruption where that could be dangerous. A direct-on-line (DOL) starter does both: a contactor held in by its coil (it drops out when the supply fails and needs the start button pressed again) and a thermal overload relay. A plain rotary isolator would restart the machine as soon as the power came back.
  • 553, plugs, sockets and couplers: Table 55.1 lists plug types, ratings and standards. On a cable coupler, the supply side must be the female part, so live pins are never exposed.
  • 554, heaters: water heaters need an overheat cut-out as well as the thermostat (an immersion heater's reset button).
  • 555, transformers: including autotransformers; a step-up autotransformer mustn't be used on an IT system.
  • 557, auxiliary circuits: control circuits, such as a light switch driving a contactor coil that switches a bigger load.
  • 559, luminaires: lights in curtain pelmets mustn't be harmed by the curtains; luminaires must suit their surroundings (a floodlight near a thatched roof); through wiring is only allowed where the luminaire is designed for it; display stands; and a table in Section 559 explains the symbols marked on luminaires and their control gear.

Chapter 56: safety services

Fire alarms, emergency lighting, and supplies that must keep running, such as essential supplies in hospitals. Their circuits must be separate from other circuits (560.7). Overload protection may be left out where losing the supply would be more dangerous, but the overload must then be indicated. Drawings of the safety services must be kept at the origin. Where the system must work during a fire, the cable must be fire-resistant (such as FP200) and fixed so it doesn't collapse (560.8). Follow the designer's specification, and get any change agreed by the designer.

Chapter 57: stationary secondary batteries

New as a chapter in Amendment 4 (it was a section before): rechargeable battery systems that stay in one place, such as home storage batteries with solar PV.
