Supply and distribution

AC and three-phase

What "alternating" really means, what the 230 V on the label actually measures, and how a power station makes three supplies at once.

Key takeaways

UK mains is AC at 50 Hz: the voltage follows a sine wave, and one full cycle (360°, or 2π radians) takes 20 ms. "230 V" is the RMS (effective) value; the actual peaks are about ±325 V (230 × √2). Generators make three-phase AC: three sine waves a third of a cycle (120°) apart, on L1 (brown), L2 (black) and L3 (grey). Each line is 230 V to neutral and 400 V to another line (230 × √3). At every instant the three add up to zero, so a balanced load puts almost no current in the neutral. Most homes have a single-phase supply (one line and neutral, usually with a 60 to 100 A main fuse); larger buildings and big loads use three-phase.

Alternating current

A battery gives direct current (DC): a steady voltage, so current always flows the same way. The mains supply is alternating current: the voltage swings positive, back through zero, negative, and back again, so the current keeps reversing direction.

In the UK it completes 50 cycles a second, a frequency of 50 hertz (Hz). So one cycle takes T = 1f = 150 = 0.02 s, or 20 ms.

The shape is a sine wave, because it comes from a spinning generator. One full cycle is one full turn: 360°, which mathematicians also call 2π radians.

Why "230 V" isn't the peak

The voltage is only at its peak for an instant, and spends a lot of each cycle near zero. So the peak would overstate what the supply can actually do. Instead, AC is described by its RMS (root mean square) value: the DC voltage that would produce the same heating in a resistor.

For a sine wave, VRMS = Vpeak√2, so Vpeak = 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V. Every mains voltage you'll see quoted, including 230 V and 400 V, is an RMS value, and meters read RMS too. But insulation has to withstand the 325 V peaks, not just the 230 V average.

How three-phase is made

A power station generator has a magnet (the rotor) spinning inside three fixed coils (the stator), spaced a third of a turn (120°) apart. As the magnet turns, the magnetic field through each coil rises, falls and reverses. That changing field induces an alternating voltage in the coil.

Each coil gets the same wave, but a third of a turn later than the one before. A coil's voltage is biggest when the field through it is changing fastest: as the poles sweep across it, not when a pole points straight at it. A two-pole generator spinning at 3,000 revolutions per minute makes 50 turns a second, which gives 50 Hz.

Three-phase supplies and the star point

The grid is three-phase: three separate AC supplies, called L1, L2 and L3, each a third of a cycle (120°) out of step with the others. At the local substation, the transformer's three secondary windings are joined at one end. That junction is the star point.

  • The neutral conductor comes from the star point.
  • The star point is connected to earth at the substation, through an earth electrode. That's what ties the whole supply to earth potential.
  • Between any line and neutral there's 230 V. Between any two lines there's 400 V (230 × √3 ≈ 400).
  • Most homes get one line plus neutral. Neighbouring houses are spread across L1, L2 and L3 to balance the load.
  • The three line conductors are coloured brown (L1), black (L2) and grey (L3). Older installations used red, yellow and blue.

Single-phase and three-phase supplies

Most homes have a single-phase supply: one line conductor and a neutral, taken from one of the three phases at the local substation. That gives 230 V, and the main fuse in the cut-out is usually 60, 80 or 100 A. At 100 A that's about 23 kW, plenty for a typical house.

A three-phase supply brings all three lines plus the neutral. It's used for larger buildings and heavy loads:

  • More power through the same size of cable. Three lines at 100 A each can deliver about three times the power of a single-phase supply.
  • Motors. Three phases a third of a cycle apart make a smoothly rotating magnetic field, so three-phase motors start themselves and run smoothly. The phase order matters: swap two lines and a three-phase motor runs backwards. The standard order is L1, L2, L3 (brown, black, grey).
  • Big domestic loads. Fast 22 kW car chargers and larger heat pumps need three-phase, so it's turning up in more homes.
  • Balance. Spreading loads evenly across the three phases keeps the neutral current small.
