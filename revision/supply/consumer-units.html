Consumer units and protective devices

Every circuit in a home starts at the consumer unit. Here's what's inside it, how each protective device works, and why the way they're arranged decides how much of the house goes dark when something trips.

Key takeaways

A consumer unit holds the main switch (a double-pole isolator, not a protective device), the busbars, neutral and earth bars, and a protective device for every circuit. In homes it must have a non-combustible (usually steel) enclosure. An MCB protects against overcurrent: a thermal trip handles slow overloads and a magnetic trip handles short circuits and earth faults almost instantly. Types B, C and D trip instantly at 3–5, 5–10 and 10–20 times their rating. An RCD (a stand-alone one is an RCCB) compares line and neutral current and trips on an imbalance, 30 mA for additional protection, but gives no overcurrent protection at all. An RCBO does both jobs for one circuit. On a split-load board one RCD covers several circuits, so one fault turns them all off; with an RCBO per circuit only the faulty one goes off. Press the RCD test buttons every six months.

Overview

Inside a consumer unit for a small flat

Tap the main switch or an RCBO to switch it.

Follow the supply from the right. The meter tails bring line and neutral into the top of the main switch. Its line output feeds the busbar along the bottom, which supplies every RCBO from below. Its neutral output feeds the neutral bar at the top. Each circuit's cable comes in from the top: the line and neutral connect to the top of that circuit's RCBO, and the CPC goes to the shared earth bar, which connects through the earthing conductor to the supply's earth. Each RCBO also has a flying neutral lead to the neutral bar. The circuit's own neutral goes into the RCBO (not to the bar), so the RCBO can compare that circuit's line and neutral currents. Where a wire crosses a bar without a dot, it isn't connected to it. Layouts vary between manufacturers, and in a flat the cables may come in from below instead.

What's in a consumer unit

  • Main switch: a double-pole switch (it breaks line and neutral) that isolates the whole installation, typically rated 100 A. It's for switching off, not for protection: it won't trip on a fault.
  • Busbars: copper bars that carry the supply from the main switch to each device.
  • Protective devices: one per circuit, such as MCBs or RCBOs, and sometimes RCDs covering groups of them.
  • Neutral bar(s): where each circuit's neutral connects. On a split-load board there's one per RCD, and a circuit's neutral must go to the bar for the RCD that protects it.
  • Earth bar: where every circuit's CPC connects, linked to the main earthing terminal.

Layout. It varies between manufacturers, but in UK consumer units the main switch is commonly on the right, and the circuits are numbered outwards from it, right to left. The highest-rated circuits, such as the cooker or shower, usually go nearest the main switch and lighting furthest away. The circuit chart on the door lists them in the same numbered order.

Fires starting in plastic consumer units led to a rule, since 2016, that consumer units in homes must have an enclosure made of non-combustible material, usually steel (or sit inside a non-combustible cabinet). Many also include a surge protective device (SPD), which diverts voltage spikes from lightning and switching to earth. Some include arc fault detection devices (AFDDs), which spot the electrical signature of a dangerous arc, such as a loose connection or damaged cable, and disconnect before it starts a fire. BS 7671 calls for them in some higher-risk buildings and recommends them in others.

Fuses

The oldest protective device is a fuse: a thin conductor that melts and breaks the circuit when too much current flows. You'll meet old rewirable fuses (a length of fuse wire in a carrier, BS 3036) in older homes, cartridge fuses in older fuse boards and cut-outs, and the 3 A or 13 A cartridge fuse in every UK plug (BS 1362). A blown fuse has to be replaced, which is one reason MCBs took over.

MCBs: miniature circuit breakers

An MCB protects a circuit's cables against overcurrent, using two mechanisms in one:

  • Thermal trip (overload): a bimetallic strip, two metals bonded together that expand at different rates. Current heats it, it bends, and when it bends far enough it releases the trip. The bigger the overload, the faster it trips: a small overload might take an hour, a large one a few seconds. This copes with harmless short surges, like a motor starting.
  • Magnetic trip (short circuit or earth fault): a coil that the current flows through. A large fault current makes a magnetic field strong enough to pull a plunger and trip the breaker within milliseconds.

The type letter says how big a current the magnetic trip needs, as a multiple of the rating (In). The higher types ride through the large start-up surges of motors and transformers:

TypeInstant (magnetic) tripTypical use
B3 to 5 × InHomes and general circuits: the usual choice
C5 to 10 × InMotors, fluorescent lighting, commercial loads with switch-on surges
D10 to 20 × InBig inrush loads: large motors, transformers, welders

Higher types need a lower earth fault loop impedance to trip in time, because the fault current has to be bigger (see the earth fault loop explorer). Domestic MCBs are usually rated to break fault currents up to 6,000 A (6 kA).

RCDs: residual current devices

An RCD doesn't measure how much current flows. It compares the current going out on the line with the current coming back on the neutral. In a healthy circuit they're equal. If some current leaks to earth, perhaps through a person, less comes back on the neutral. That difference is the residual current.

Inside, line and neutral both pass through a ring-shaped iron core (a toroid). When the currents are equal, their magnetic fields cancel. An imbalance leaves a field in the core, which induces a current in a small sensing coil, and that trips the device.

  • For additional protection, the rated residual current (IΔn) is 30 mA. A general-type RCD must trip within 300 ms at 30 mA, and within 40 ms at five times that (150 mA).
  • An RCD gives no overcurrent protection. An overload, or a short circuit between line and neutral, is balanced, so the RCD doesn't see it. A stand-alone RCD, an RCCB, always needs MCBs or fuses as well.
  • It also can't protect someone touching line and neutral at the same time, because that current is balanced too.
  • Press the test button every six months. It creates a deliberate small imbalance to prove the mechanism still works. (It doesn't measure the trip time; that's an RCD test with an instrument.)
  • RCD types AC, A, F and B differ in which kinds of leakage they detect (see DC and AC).

RCBOs: both in one

An RCBO (residual current breaker with overcurrent protection) combines an MCB and an RCD in one device, protecting a single circuit against overload, short circuit and earth leakage. Because each circuit has its own, an earth fault only turns off that circuit. They cost more, but they've become the usual choice for new and replacement consumer units.

Split-load and RCBO boards

On a split-load (or dual-RCD) board, the circuits are divided into two groups, each fed through one RCD and protected individually by MCBs. It's cheaper, but one fault, or a few small leakages adding up across several circuits, trips the RCD and takes the whole group off, perhaps half the lights and sockets in the house. Spreading circuits sensibly between the two (upstairs lights with downstairs sockets, say) means a trip never leaves anyone in complete darkness. An RCBO board avoids the problem: only the faulty circuit goes off.
