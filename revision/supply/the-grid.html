The grid and transformers

How electricity gets from a power station to a socket, why it travels at 400,000 volts on the way, and the job transformers do.

Key takeaways

Electricity is generated at around 20 kV, stepped up to 400 kV or 275 kV for transmission, then down in stages (132, 33 and 11 kV) to 400/230 V at your local substation. High voltage means low current, and so far lower I²R losses in the lines. A transformer changes voltage by its turns ratio (VsVp = NsNp). Power in roughly equals power out, so stepping the voltage down steps the current up. Transformers only work on AC, which is a big reason the grid uses it.

The journey

The grid carries 50 Hz alternating current, made by three-phase generators at power stations (see Three-phase supplies for how that works). Between the generator and your socket, the voltage is changed several times by transformers.

The journey

From power station to plug

Exact voltages vary by region, but this is the usual chain. The voltage is stepped down in stages, not in one jump. Your local substation (the green box at the end of the street, or a pole-mounted transformer in the country) takes 11 kV down to 400 V three-phase, which gives 230 V to each home.

Why such high voltages?

Power lines have resistance, and current through resistance makes heat: P = I² × R, from the Power lesson. That heat is wasted energy.

For the same power, P = V × I. Raise the voltage and the current needed goes down by the same factor. Because losses depend on I², 10 times the voltage means 1/100th of the losses.

How a transformer works

A transformer is two coils of wire wound on the same iron core. The coils don't touch electrically.

  1. AC in the primary coil creates a magnetic field in the core. Because the current keeps reversing, the field keeps reversing too.
  2. A changing magnetic field passing through a coil induces a voltage in it. So the reversing field induces an AC voltage in the secondary coil.
  3. The voltage depends on the number of turns. Fewer turns on the secondary means a lower voltage (step down); more turns means a higher voltage (step up).

VsVp = NsNp

Power in ≈ power out (real transformers are around 98–99% efficient). So when the voltage goes down, the current goes up by the same factor.

With DC, the field in the core is steady. A steady field induces nothing, so the secondary gets no voltage. Try it below.
