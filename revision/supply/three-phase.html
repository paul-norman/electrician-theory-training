Supply and distribution

Three-phase supplies

How a power station makes three supplies at once, how they reach a building, and when a building gets one phase or all three.

Key takeaways

Power stations make three-phase AC: a magnet spinning inside three coils spaced 120° apart gives three sine waves a third of a cycle apart, on L1 (brown), L2 (black) and L3 (grey). A coil's voltage peaks when the field through it is changing fastest. At the local substation the transformer's three windings meet at the star point, which provides the neutral and is connected to earth. Each line is 230 V to neutral and 400 V to another line (230 × √3). At every instant the three voltages add up to zero, so a balanced load puts almost no current in the neutral. Most homes have a single-phase supply (one line and neutral, usually with a 60 to 100 A main fuse); larger buildings and big loads such as motors use all three phases.

How three-phase is made

A power station generator has a magnet (the rotor) spinning inside three fixed coils (the stator), spaced a third of a turn (120°) apart. As the magnet turns, the magnetic field through each coil rises, falls and reverses. That changing field induces an alternating voltage in the coil.

(For what a sine wave, RMS and 50 Hz mean, see DC and AC.) Each coil gets the same wave, but a third of a turn later than the one before. A coil's voltage is biggest when the field through it is changing fastest: as the poles sweep across it, not when a pole points straight at it. A two-pole generator spinning at 3,000 revolutions per minute makes 50 turns a second, which gives 50 Hz.

Three-phase supplies and the star point

The grid is three-phase: three separate AC supplies, called L1, L2 and L3, each a third of a cycle (120°) out of step with the others. At the local substation, the transformer's three secondary windings are joined at one end. That junction is the star point.

  • The neutral conductor comes from the star point.
  • The star point is connected to earth at the substation, through an earth electrode. That's what ties the whole supply to earth potential.
  • Between any line and neutral there's 230 V. Between any two lines there's 400 V (230 × √3 ≈ 400).
  • Most homes get one line plus neutral. Neighbouring houses are spread across L1, L2 and L3 to balance the load.
  • The three line conductors are coloured brown (L1), black (L2) and grey (L3). Older installations used red, yellow and blue.

Single-phase and three-phase supplies

Most homes have a single-phase supply: one line conductor and a neutral, taken from one of the three phases at the local substation. That gives 230 V, and the main fuse in the cut-out is usually 60, 80 or 100 A. At 100 A that's about 23 kW, plenty for a typical house.

A three-phase supply brings all three lines plus the neutral. It's used for larger buildings and heavy loads:

  • More power through the same size of cable. Three lines at 100 A each can deliver about three times the power of a single-phase supply.
  • Motors. Three phases a third of a cycle apart make a smoothly rotating magnetic field, so three-phase motors start themselves and run smoothly. The phase order matters: swap two lines and a three-phase motor runs backwards. The standard order is L1, L2, L3 (brown, black, grey).
  • Big domestic loads. Fast 22 kW car chargers and larger heat pumps need three-phase, so it's turning up in more homes.
  • Balance. Spreading loads evenly across the three phases keeps the neutral current small.
