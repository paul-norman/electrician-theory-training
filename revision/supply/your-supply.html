Your supply: conductors and earthing

Where the network's equipment ends and the installation begins, what each conductor actually does, and why earth matters for safety.

Key takeaways

The DNO owns the service cable and the sealed cut-out with its main fuse. The meter belongs to the energy supplier, and the installation starts at the meter tails. Line (brown) carries current to the load. Neutral (blue) brings it back to the star point and must always be treated as live. The circuit protective conductor (CPC, green/yellow, often just called "the earth") carries current only during a fault. Current always returns to its source, so touching a line conductor while standing on the ground completes a loop through you, the ground and the transformer.

Who owns what

The supply into a building passes through equipment owned by three different parties. As an electrician, you only work on the consumer's side.

Hands off the network's equipment

The cut-out and meter are sealed. Breaking a seal or interfering with them is a criminal offence, and dangerous: the cut-out is live all the time, and the only protection upstream of it is the DNO's fuse back at the substation. If a job needs the supply isolated at the cut-out, the DNO (or the supplier, for the meter) has to do it. That's why many installations have an isolator fitted after the meter: it gives the electrician a safe way to switch off.

The mains position

The mains position is where the supply enters the building and the installation begins. The Electrician's Guide to the Building Regulations covers it in Chapter 3, and the On-Site Guide has similar drawings. In order, you'll find:

  1. The service cable, owned by the DNO. On new homes it often rises through a curved duct (a "hockey stick") into a meter box on the outside wall.
  2. The cut-out, also the DNO's, holding the main fuse: usually 60, 80 or 100 A, never more than 100 A for a typical home. On a supply taken from a three-phase network, it's often labelled with the phase used (L1, L2 or L3).
  3. The meter, owned by the energy supplier, not the DNO.
  4. The meter tails, the consumer's, running to the consumer unit.
  5. Ideally, a double-pole isolator in the tails, just after the meter.
  6. The consumer unit, with the main earthing terminal (MET) inside it or in a separate block nearby.

The ESQCR require the DNO to put its cut-out and meter position somewhere safe, protected from mechanical damage, and where they can be maintained safely.

Why an isolator matters. Without one, the only way to switch off the whole installation, for example to replace the consumer unit, is to ask the DNO to withdraw its fuse, or ask the supplier to fit an isolator. Pulling the cut-out fuse yourself is a criminal offence, however often you see it done. And it doesn't fully isolate anyway: the fuse is only in the line. The neutral passes through the cut-out on a solid link, so it stays connected. A double-pole isolator breaks both line and neutral. Once the installation has been made safe, fitting one (in the consumer's tails) makes every future job safer. Like a main switch, an isolator has a current rating but gives no overcurrent protection: it won't trip if that rating is exceeded.

Henley blocks. To feed a second consumer unit, for example for an extension or a loft conversion when the existing board is full, the tails can be split with a pair of Henley blocks: heavy-duty connector blocks, one for line and one for neutral, each with an incoming tail and two outgoing ones.

The three conductors

Line (L): brown Carries current from the supply to the load, at 230 V relative to earth. Switches and fuses always go in the line conductor, so switching off leaves the load disconnected from the live supply. Before 2004: red
Neutral (N): blue Carries the current back from the load to the transformer's star point. In a simple circuit it carries the same current as the line. Normally close to 0 V, because the star point is earthed. Before 2004: black
Circuit protective conductor (CPC): green and yellow Connects exposed metalwork (metal cases, back boxes) to earth. Carries no current in normal use. In a fault it carries enough current to trip the breaker fast. Often called "the earth" or "earth wire" on site

Neutral: "0 V", but treat it as live

You'll often hear that the neutral is at 0 V. That's true when everything is healthy, but you can't rely on it:

  • Current flowing in the neutral causes a small voltage drop along it, so it sits a few volts above earth.
  • If the neutral is broken anywhere, everything on the load side of the break can sit at the full 230 V, through the load.
  • On shared neutrals or wrongly connected circuits, a neutral can carry current from another circuit.

BS 7671 counts the neutral as a live conductor. You'll isolate and prove dead on line and neutral, every time.

What earth really does

The ground is huge, so adding or removing a bit of charge doesn't change its potential. That makes it a convenient 0 V reference: "230 V" means 230 V relative to earth.

A common myth is that current "wants to go to earth" and disappears into the ground. It doesn't. Current always tries to return to its source. For the mains, the source is the transformer, and its star point is connected to earth. So the ground becomes a path back to the transformer. That's how you can get a shock just by touching a line conductor while standing on the floor.
