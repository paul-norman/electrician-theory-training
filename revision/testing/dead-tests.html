Dead tests

The first tests are done with the installation safely isolated. They prove the protective conductors are continuous, the insulation is sound and everything is connected the right way round, before any voltage goes on.

Key takeaways

Dead tests come first, in order: continuity of protective conductors (including bonding), continuity of ring final circuits, insulation resistance, then polarity. Continuity is measured with a low-resistance ohmmeter, with its leads nulled. Method 1 links line to CPC at the board and measures R1 + R2 at each point. The ring final test has three steps: end-to-end readings (r1, rn, r2), then line cross-connected to neutral, then line cross-connected to CPC. Every socket should read about r1 + r24 on step 3, and the highest reading is recorded as R1 + R2. Insulation resistance on 230 V circuits is tested at 500 V DC and must be at least 1 MΩ (250 V DC and 0.5 MΩ for SELV and PELV). Circuits in parallel lower the overall reading. Polarity checks that every single-pole switch and device is in the line conductor.

The order of tests

Guidance Note 3 and BS 7671 Part 6 give the sequence. The order matters: each test relies on the ones before it. You can't trust an insulation test, or a live test, until you know the protective conductors are connected.

  1. Continuity of protective conductors, including main and supplementary bonding
  2. Continuity of ring final circuit conductors
  3. Insulation resistance
  4. Protection by SELV, PELV or electrical separation, and by barriers and enclosures, where used
  5. Polarity
  6. Then the live tests. The very first, as soon as the supply is connected, is to check the supply polarity at the origin. Then earth electrode resistance, earth fault loop impedance, prospective fault current, RCDs, functional tests (see Live tests)

If any test fails, put it right and repeat that test and any earlier ones the fault could have affected.

Continuity of protective conductors

Use a low-resistance ohmmeter, and null the leads first (touch them together and zero the meter), so their own resistance isn't added to every reading. If you can't null them, subtract the lead resistance: with 1.50 Ω of leads, a reading of 1.51 Ω means the conductor itself is 0.01 Ω. There are two methods:

  • Method 1 (R1 + R2). At the board, link the circuit's line conductor to its CPC with a short lead. Then measure between line and earth at every point on the circuit. Each reading is the resistance of the line conductor and CPC to that point, R1 + R2. The highest, usually at the far end, is recorded. It also checks polarity as you go, and it's the value you need for Zs.
  • Method 2 (R2 only). Use a long "wander lead" from the main earthing terminal, and measure from it to the earth terminal at each point. That gives R2 on its own. It's also how you test main and supplementary bonding conductors: one lead at each end of the conductor. Disconnect the bonding conductor at the MET first (with the installation isolated, and reconnect it straight after). Otherwise the test current can find a way back through the other bonded services: water and gas pipes both meet at a boiler, for example. You'd then get a good reading even if the conductor you're testing were broken. Bond after the meter or stopcock, within 600 mm of where the service enters.

Check for parallel paths: metal conduit, bonded pipework or a second earth connection can give a low reading even when the CPC itself is broken.

Working out expected resistances: GN3 Table B1

Guidance Note 3, Appendix B, Table B1 (also in the On-Site Guide, Appendix I) gives copper conductor resistance in mΩ per metre at 20 °C. Read its first column as "conductor 1" and the second as "conductor 2", rather than line and protective conductor. A size with an asterisk is twin and earth, and the pair next to it shows the CPC that comes in that cable: 1.0/1.0, 1.5/1.0, 2.5/1.5, 4/1.5, 6/2.5, 10/4 and 16/6. The value on a pair row is the two conductors added together, which is what an R1 + R2 reading measures.

R = mΩ/m × length1000   and   length = 1000 × RmΩ/m

  • One conductor, such as a 15 m, 4 mm² supplementary bonding conductor: use the single 4 mm² value, 4.61 mΩ/m, not the 4/1.5 pair (9.22 is the trap: that's two 4 mm² conductors). R = 4.61 × 151000 = 0.069 Ω.
  • A twin and earth circuit's R1 + R2, 20 m of 2.5 mm²: the 2.5/1.5 pair, 19.51 mΩ/m. R = 19.51 × 201000 = 0.39 Ω.
  • Length from a reading, 4 mm² twin and earth measuring 0.62 Ω: the 4/1.5 pair is 16.71 mΩ/m, so length = 1000 × 0.6216.71 = 37.1 m. Handy for fault-finding.

Real conductors run warmer than 20 °C, so later tables apply a multiplier for design work; for checking test readings, the 20 °C values are what you compare against. Circuits wired in singles usually have a CPC the same size as the line (Table 54.7 makes that the easy choice), so use the matching single size twice.

Continuity of ring final circuits

A ring final circuit starts and ends at the board, so it has two "legs" of each conductor. If the ring is broken somewhere, every socket still works, fed from one side only, but the cable can be overloaded. The three-step test finds that, and gives you R1 + R2.

  1. End to end. At the board, separate the two legs and measure each conductor's loop: line to line (r1), neutral to neutral (rn) and CPC to CPC (r2). r1 and rn should be within 0.05 Ω of each other. In 2.5/1.5 mm² twin and earth, the CPC is thinner, so r2 is about 1.67 times r1.
  2. Line to neutral, cross-connected. Join the line of one leg to the neutral of the other, and vice versa. Measure line to neutral at every socket. Each reading should be about r1 + rn4, all within 0.05 Ω of each other. Spurs read higher.
  3. Line to CPC, cross-connected. Do the same with line and CPC, and measure line to earth at every socket. Each should be about r1 + r24. The highest reading is the circuit's R1 + R2.

Why a quarter? Cross-connecting turns the ring into a loop twice as long, and wherever you measure on it, you're looking at two equal halves of that loop in parallel. Twice the length, then halved twice, gives a quarter.

Reading the faults. A broken ring shows up in step 1 as an open circuit, and must be fixed before steps 2 and 3. If the readings in step 2 or 3 start low, rise towards the middle of the ring and fall again, the legs have been joined the wrong way (same end to same end): swap them. Reversed polarity at a socket doesn't show in step 2, but shows in step 3 as a very high or open reading at that socket.

Insulation resistance

This proves the insulation between conductors, and between conductors and earth, is sound. An insulation resistance tester applies a DC test voltage and measures the tiny current that leaks through. BS 7671 gives the test voltage and minimum value:

Circuit nominal voltageTest voltage (DC)Minimum insulation resistance
SELV and PELV250 V0.5 MΩ
Up to and including 500 V (including 230 V and 400 V)500 V1.0 MΩ
Above 500 V1,000 V1.0 MΩ

New circuits usually read off the top of the meter's scale (often over 200 MΩ), so a reading that passes but is much lower than expected is worth investigating.

Before you test, protect anything that could be damaged or give a false reading: unplug or disconnect equipment, and disconnect or isolate electronic devices such as dimmers, timers, smoke alarms and surge protective devices. Close switches and circuit breakers so the whole circuit is tested, and operate two-way switches to test both strappers. Test between line and neutral, and between the live conductors (joined) and earth. Where electronic devices can't be disconnected, join line and neutral and test only to earth, at 250 V if necessary.

The order GN3 gives: with loads removed, test line to neutral at 500 V, then line and neutral joined, to earth at 500 V. Once the voltage-sensitive equipment is reconnected, a further test from line and neutral joined to earth at 250 V is acceptable, because both conductors are at the same voltage and nothing flows through the equipment. Check the tester first: leads apart should read off the scale and leads together near zero. Many electricians start at 250 V in case something's been missed: a lamp left in place reads almost nothing line to neutral and glows from the test voltage (try it on the Dead test bench).

Testing a whole board at once: with the main switch off and every circuit breaker on, testing between the neutral bar and the line busbar covers all the circuits together. That only works with plain circuit breakers; with RCBOs, each circuit has to be tested on its own.

Recording: for continuity, record the highest reading (the worst); for insulation resistance, the lowest. The schedule of test results has a column for the test voltage and two for readings: live to live (L–N on single-phase; every combination of L1, L2, L3 and N on three-phase) and live to earth. GN3 notes that although 1 MΩ meets BS 7671, a new installation shouldn't give a result anywhere near that low.

Circuits in parallel. Testing at the board measures all the circuits at once, and their insulation resistances combine like resistors in parallel (see Series and parallel circuits). So the whole-installation reading is always lower than the lowest single circuit. Eight circuits at 10 MΩ each give 108 = 1.25 MΩ. Extending a circuit adds more cable in parallel, so its insulation resistance goes down.

Ring and insulation questions

Polarity

Polarity testing confirms that:

  • every fuse and single-pole switch or circuit breaker is in the line conductor only
  • the centre contact of Edison screw lampholders is connected to the line conductor (unless the lampholder is a type that doesn't need it)
  • socket-outlets and other accessories are wired the right way round

It's done dead, usually alongside the R1 + R2 test: with line linked to CPC at the board, operating a light switch makes the reading appear and disappear, proving the switch is in the line. It's confirmed live later: at the origin, line to earth and line to neutral should read the supply voltage, and neutral to earth almost nothing.
