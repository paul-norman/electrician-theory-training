Inspection and certification

New work is checked and certified before it's put into service. Existing installations are inspected from time to time and reported on. Different jobs, different paperwork.

Key takeaways

Initial verification (BS 7671 Chapter 64) checks new work, by inspection and then testing, before it's energised and used. It's recorded on an EIC (new installations, new circuits, consumer unit changes) or a MEIWC (an addition or alteration without a new circuit), given to the person who ordered the work. An EIC has three signatures: designer, installer and inspector (a sole trader signs all three; the single-signature form has been withdrawn). It needs a set of schedules for each board. Periodic inspection (Chapter 65) reports on an existing installation's condition on an EICR, within an agreed extent and limitations. Each observation is coded: C1 danger present, C2 potentially dangerous, C3 improvement recommended, FI further investigation required. Any C1 or C2 makes the report unsatisfactory; under Amendment 4, an FI no longer does so automatically. Guidance Note 3 is the reference book for all three inspection and testing exams.

Inspection comes first

Inspection is done before testing, usually with the installation isolated. You're checking that the equipment is the right type, correctly selected and installed, and not damaged. The schedule of inspections on the certificate lists the headings, and the inspector is responsible for everything each heading covers. Some typical items:

  • the method of protection against electric shock, and its parts: barriers, enclosures, earthing, bonding and RCDs
  • cables of the right size and type, properly supported, routed in safe zones or protected, and with fire barriers restored where they pass through walls and floors
  • connections tight, conductors identified, and sheaths taken inside accessories
  • devices for isolation and switching in the right places, and labelled
  • IP ratings suited to the location, and special locations meeting Part 7
  • required notices and labels: RCD test notices, periodic inspection notices, and warnings where there are other sources such as solar panels

Use every sense: look for damage and scorching, feel for heat, listen for buzzing, and notice any smell of burning.

An EIC has room for three signatures, for the designer, the installer (construction) and the inspector, because they may be different people with different responsibilities. The old single-signature EIC has been withdrawn, so a sole trader simply signs all three parts. Certificates go to the person who ordered the work (the client, GN3 1.3); keep a copy for your records, which a competent person scheme will ask to see at its annual assessment. Notifying building control is a separate matter (see Part P and notification).

Filling in the certificates

  • One EIC per job, one set of schedules per board. A house with a garage sub-board gets one EIC and two sets of schedules (circuit details and test results); a school with ten boards gets one EIC and ten. The EIC isn't valid without them.
  • Every job gets certified, however small. Replacing a broken light switch is new work on that circuit, so it's tested and given an MEIWC. (Changing a lamp or plugging in an appliance isn't installation work.)
  • One MEIWC per circuit. Adding sockets and changing the lighting in one bedroom touches two circuits, so it needs two minor works certificates.
  • The designer sets the interval to the first periodic inspection, recorded on the EIC. If a question asks who decides it, the certificate itself gives the answer.
  • Write N/A rather than leaving a box blank. Use a unique certificate number for your own records.
  • The IET model forms are free to download. Completed examples are in GN3 Chapter 5 and the blank forms in BS 7671 Appendix 6. Software and scheme forms (NICEIC, NAPIT) are based on them. You don't have to belong to a competent person scheme to issue an EIC: certifying is for all work, while the scheme only lets you notify building control yourself (see Part P and notification). A scheme may cover you for domestic work only, in which case its forms can't be used for commercial jobs.

Supply characteristics on the EIC

Page 2 of the EIC records what GN3 2.3 calls the required information:

  • Earthing arrangement, such as TN-C-S (PME).
  • Number and type of live conductors: AC, and "1-phase, 2-wire" for a normal home (line and neutral) or "3-phase, 4-wire" (L1, L2, L3 and N). The earthing conductor isn't counted: it isn't a live conductor.
  • Nominal voltage U/U0: written in the order the form asks, so 400/230 V for three-phase (U between lines, U0 to earth), or 230 V single-phase. Frequency 50 Hz.
  • Prospective fault current and Ze, by enquiry to the network operator or by measurement.
  • Supply protective device: type and rating from the label on the cut-out fuse carrier. Never pull the network operator's fuse to look; say in the comments that you've taken it from the label.
  • Main switch: its standard (often BS EN 60947-3), number of poles, and current rating, such as 100 A. It's an on-load isolator, not overcurrent protection.
  • Maximum demand, after diversity. Adding up the circuit breaker ratings almost always comes to more than the 100 A supply, so use diversity instead (see the calculator below and Design current and diversity).

Who's responsible for what (GN3 2.1)

The inspector checks the work against the design but doesn't redo it. For example, you'd check at the board that the cable sizes and device ratings match the designer's circuit schedule. You wouldn't redo the cable-sizing calculations (that's the designer's job), or check that the size is kept along the whole run (that's the installer's). Inspection confirms other people's work; it doesn't take their responsibility away or fix their problems.

Adding to an existing installation

Before adding a circuit, check the existing installation can take it (BS 7671 132.16): the cut-out and meter in good condition, the earthing and bonding in place, and a consumer unit fit to extend. You certify only your own work, but if you notice other defects, tell the client and note them in the comments, including any they decline to have fixed. Anything immediately dangerous must be made safe. Some things to look out for:

  • A meter with its cover missing, or a consumer unit with a missing blank, leaves live parts exposed: C1 on a condition report, and the energy supplier must be called for the meter.
  • Bitumen dripping from an old cut-out means it's overheating. Get the network operator to look at it.
  • A plastic consumer unit in good condition can take a new circuit; a cracked or heat-damaged one can't.

Test at first fix, too

Inspection and testing also happen during construction (GN3 2.4). After first fix, before plastering, join the cable ends (lever connectors are handy) and test continuity and insulation resistance on each circuit, then record the results and give the client a copy. If a cable is later cut or screwed through by another trade, your earlier readings show it was sound when you left. Repeat all the tests at second fix and compare.

EICR codes

C1Danger presentRisk of injury now. Immediate remedial action: make it safe on the spot, and tell the person responsible.
C2Potentially dangerousNot dangerous this minute, but would become so if a fault or another event happened. Urgent remedial action.
C3Improvement recommendedNot a source of danger, but improving it would make the installation safer. Often an older installation that met the rules of its day.
FIFurther investigationSomething needs investigating without delay, but the inspection couldn't establish what. Rarely needed in a house.

The overall result is unsatisfactory if there's any C1 or C2. With only C3s, it can be satisfactory. Amendment 4 of BS 7671 (2026) changed two things in Chapter 65: an FI code no longer makes the report unsatisfactory on its own, and the inspector must take account of the notes in Appendix 6 and include guidance for the person receiving the report. Photos and thermal images can be attached as evidence.

Electrical Safety First's Best Practice Guide 4 gives typical codes for common findings, and is allowed into the periodic inspection practical. The coding is still the inspector's judgement.

How often?

Guidance Note 3 recommends maximum intervals between inspections. The inspector sets the actual date on the report, depending on the installation's condition and use. Some examples:
