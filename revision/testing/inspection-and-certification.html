Inspection and certification

New work is checked and certified before it's put into service. Existing installations are inspected from time to time and reported on. Different jobs, different paperwork.

Key takeaways

Initial verification (BS 7671 Chapter 64) checks new work, by inspection and then testing, before it's energised and used. It's recorded on an EIC (new installations, new circuits, consumer unit changes) or a MEIWC (an addition or alteration without a new circuit), given to the person who ordered the work. An EIC can have one signature, or three: designer, installer and inspector. Periodic inspection (Chapter 65) reports on an existing installation's condition on an EICR, within an agreed extent and limitations. Each observation is coded: C1 danger present, C2 potentially dangerous, C3 improvement recommended, FI further investigation required. Any C1 or C2 makes the report unsatisfactory; under Amendment 4, an FI no longer does so automatically. Guidance Note 3 is the reference book for all three inspection and testing exams.

Inspection comes first

Inspection is done before testing, usually with the installation isolated. You're checking that the equipment is the right type, correctly selected and installed, and not damaged. The schedule of inspections on the certificate lists the headings, and the inspector is responsible for everything each heading covers. Some typical items:

  • the method of protection against electric shock, and its parts: barriers, enclosures, earthing, bonding and RCDs
  • cables of the right size and type, properly supported, routed in safe zones or protected, and with fire barriers restored where they pass through walls and floors
  • connections tight, conductors identified, and sheaths taken inside accessories
  • devices for isolation and switching in the right places, and labelled
  • IP ratings suited to the location, and special locations meeting Part 7
  • required notices and labels: RCD test notices, periodic inspection notices, and warnings where there are other sources such as solar panels

Use every sense: look for damage and scorching, feel for heat, listen for buzzing, and notice any smell of burning.

An EIC has room for three signatures, for the designer, the installer (construction) and the inspector, because they may be different people with different responsibilities. A one-person job can use the single-signature version. Certificates go to the person who ordered the work. Notifying building control is a separate matter (see Part P and notification).

EICR codes

C1Danger presentRisk of injury now. Immediate remedial action: make it safe on the spot, and tell the person responsible.
C2Potentially dangerousNot dangerous this minute, but would become so if a fault or another event happened. Urgent remedial action.
C3Improvement recommendedNot a source of danger, but improving it would make the installation safer. Often an older installation that met the rules of its day.
FIFurther investigationSomething needs investigating without delay, but the inspection couldn't establish what. Rarely needed in a house.

The overall result is unsatisfactory if there's any C1 or C2. With only C3s, it can be satisfactory. Amendment 4 of BS 7671 (2026) changed two things in Chapter 65: an FI code no longer makes the report unsatisfactory on its own, and the inspector must take account of the notes in Appendix 6 and include guidance for the person receiving the report. Photos and thermal images can be attached as evidence.

Electrical Safety First's Best Practice Guide 4 gives typical codes for common findings, and is allowed into the periodic inspection practical. The coding is still the inspector's judgement.

How often?

Guidance Note 3 recommends maximum intervals between inspections. The inspector sets the actual date on the report, depending on the installation's condition and use. Some examples:

InstallationRecommended maximum interval
Home (owner-occupied)10 years, and on change of occupancy
Rented home5 years, and on change of occupancy. In England's private rented sector, a 5-yearly inspection is a legal requirement.
Commercial premises, such as shops and offices5 years
Industrial premises3 years
Swimming pools1 year
Construction site installations3 months
