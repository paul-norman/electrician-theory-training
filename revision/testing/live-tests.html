Live tests

Once the dead tests have passed, the supply goes on and the remaining tests check the things only a live installation can show: the earth path, the fault current, and whether the RCDs trip in time.

Key takeaways

Live tests follow the dead tests. On a TT system, earth electrode resistance is measured first. Ze is measured at the origin with the main switch off and the earthing conductor disconnected from the MET, to remove parallel paths. Reconnect it straight after. Zs is measured at the far end of each circuit (or worked out as Ze + (R1 + R2)) and compared with about 80% of the BS 7671 maximum. Prospective fault current is measured between line and neutral (PSCC) and line and earth (PEFC) at the origin. The larger is recorded, and every device must be able to break it. RCDs are tested at their rated residual current, IΔn, on both half-cycles: a general-type RCD must trip within 300 ms. Finally, functional tests check that switches, controls and interlocks work.

Before you start

Live testing means working near live parts, so it needs the right instrument (to GS38, see Safe isolation), a risk assessment and care. GN3 1.1 says to pay particular attention to GS38 for any test with an instrument that generates more than 50 V AC or 120 V DC (insulation resistance), or that uses the supply voltage (every test in the live part of the sequence). Continuity is the one test it doesn't flag. Even the Ze test is a live test: the installation is switched off, but your probes are on the live side of the main switch. Fence off the work area and don't leave open equipment unattended (GN3 1.1): that's what keeps other people safe, while isolation and locking off keep you safe. Loop and RCD testers deliberately make a current flow to earth for a moment. Warn people on site, and keep others away from exposed metalwork while you test.

Earth electrode resistance (TT systems)

On a TT system the installation's earth electrode is part of the fault path, so its resistance (RA) has to be measured. Guidance Note 3 describes three ways:

  • Method E1, earth electrode tester with test spikes. Drive a current spike well away from the electrode (at least 10 times the electrode's length), and a potential spike halfway between. Take readings with the potential spike at the halfway point and 10% of the distance either side. If all three agree closely, add them up, divide by three and record the average on the EIC (details of the earth electrode). It needs a lot of open ground and the electrode's length: on new work, the installer must give you that (GN3 2.3, required information). If you don't know the length, or there isn't room, use another method. E1 is the most accurate, and can even be done on an empty plot before the house is built.
  • Method E2, stakeless (clamp) tester. Clamps on the earthing conductor measure the current and voltage there, so the earthing conductor stays connected and the installation can stay live, handy for an office that can't be switched off. It only works where there are several electrodes in parallel, needs special kit, and can be less accurate.
  • Method E3, earth fault loop impedance tester. Measure between the line conductor at the origin and the electrode. It's exactly the Ze test (below): the reading is the whole loop through the transformer, the ground and the electrode, but the electrode is by far the biggest part. It needs only a multifunction tester and takes a minute, so it's by far the most common method, and good enough for an installation protected by RCDs.

For E1 and E3, isolate the supply and disconnect the earthing conductor first, so you measure the electrode alone. Remember the stability limit from Automatic disconnection: above about 200 Ω, an electrode may not be reliable. That isn't a pass/fail limit, just the point where it's considered unstable.

Ground conditions. Test when the ground is driest (or frozen), since that's the worst case. If the temporary spikes in an E1 test give poor contact, drive them deeper or water them (even with brine), but never water the electrode under test: it would read well today and badly once it dried out. Reconnect the earthing conductor before switching the installation back on.

External earth fault loop impedance, Ze

Ze is the part of the loop outside the installation (see Earthing systems). Measure it at the origin with an earth fault loop impedance tester, between the incoming line and the earthing conductor:

  • Isolate the installation at the main switch, and lock it off.
  • Disconnect the earthing conductor from the MET, so that current can't take a parallel path through the main bonding and pipework, which would give a falsely low reading.
  • Clip the tester's earth lead firmly onto the disconnected earthing conductor. Put the neutral probe on the incoming neutral first (it should be at 0 V), then the line probe on the incoming line. The tester shows the voltage; if the probes are the wrong way round, most testers sound a polarity warning and won't test, which also checks the supply polarity.
  • Take the reading, and reconnect the earthing conductor immediately. While it's off, the installation has no earth.

Compare the reading with what the DNO quotes for the system: typically 0.8 Ω for TN-S and 0.35 Ω for TN-C-S. A higher value needs investigating.

Earth fault loop impedance, Zs

Zs is the whole loop: Ze outside the installation plus the circuit's own R1 + R2. Every circuit has a Zs, and every protective device has a maximum Zs: keep under it and the device disconnects in time. GN3 gives two ways to verify it, and prefers the calculation: Zs = Ze + (R1 + R2), using readings you already have. Measuring it means a live test on a live circuit, often up a stepladder holding probes on live terminals while the tester works, so it's safer to avoid where you can. If you do measure, reconnect everything you disconnected first (earthing conductor, bonding), and test at the furthest point. Compare the value with about 80% of the maximum in BS 7671 Tables 41.2 to 41.4, because the conductors are cold (see Automatic disconnection).

RCDs and loop testing. A standard loop test passes a large current to earth for a few tens of milliseconds, which will trip a 30 mA RCD. Use the tester's no-trip (low-current) setting on RCD-protected circuits. Never bypass an RCD to take a reading.

Prospective fault current

Most testers measure it at the same time as Ze: they know the voltage and the impedance, so they work out I = UZ. With 237 V and Ze of 1.07 Ω, that's about 221 A.

The prospective fault current (PFC) is the biggest current that could flow in a fault at a given point. Every protective device must be able to break it safely, otherwise it could explode instead of clearing the fault. Measure at the origin (and at each distribution board):

  • PSCC, prospective short-circuit current: between line and neutral
  • PEFC, prospective earth fault current: between line and earth

Record the larger of the two as the PFC (Ipf), in kA. It can also be found by calculation or by asking the DNO: roughly I = U0Z, so a low impedance means a high fault current. With Ze of 0.03 Ω near a substation: 2300.03 ≈ 7,700 A, more than the 6 kA a typical domestic circuit breaker can break on its own. (Consumer units are tested as an assembly with the supply fuse, which is why they're commonly rated to 16 kA.) The point of measuring it: every protective device has a rated breaking capacity (Icn), printed in a rectangle on the front or side, such as 6000 (6 kA) or 10000 (10 kA). Ipf must not exceed it, or the device could explode instead of clearing the fault. Ze and Ipf are recorded on page 2 of the EIC.

Practice

Fault current questions

RCD testing

GN3 section 2.6.18 sets the order:

  1. Press the RCD's own test button first, before using the instrument. It proves the mechanism works. If it doesn't trip, stop: the circuit isn't safe, and the RCD needs replacing.
  2. With load disconnected, connect the RCD tester on the load side (at the board, or with a plug-in lead at a socket). GN3 says to use the tester's Type AC setting for every RCD type, even on a Type A.
  3. Test at ½ × IΔn (15 mA for a 30 mA device), on both half-cycles: it must not trip. If it does, the RCD is oversensitive and faulty.
  4. Test at 1 × IΔn on both the 0° and 180° half-cycles: it must trip within the time below. Record the longer of the two. (An RCD can trip at a different point of the wave depending on which half-cycle the fault starts on, which is why both are tested.)

The rest of the table in GN3 (such as tests at 5 × IΔn) is optional and useful for fault-finding. A ramp test, where the tester raises the current until the RCD trips and shows the trip current, is also optional: handy when a customer complains of nuisance tripping (a 30 mA device tripping at 27 mA is normal). Don't confuse fixed-wiring tests with in-service (PAT) testing of plug-in RCDs, which uses 5 × IΔn within 40 ms. Practise on the Live test bench.

Functional tests

The last step: check everything works as intended. Switches, isolators and circuit breakers operate; controls, interlocks and time switches do their job; RCD and AFDD test buttons work. On three-phase installations, check the phase sequence (L1, L2, L3 in the right order), so that motors turn the right way.

Supply polarity

Before relying on anything live, confirm the supply is the right way round at the origin: if it's reversed there, it's reversed everywhere, however well every circuit is wired. With an approved voltage indicator: line to neutral about 230 V, line to earth about 230 V, neutral to earth about 0 V. Line to earth reading 0 V and neutral to earth 230 V means line and neutral are swapped. A test lamp also works; a neon screwdriver doesn't. GN3's list of tests doesn't name supply polarity separately (it's confirmed during the loop test), but the On-Site Guide does, and it's the first thing to check when the supply goes on.

When something's wrong on new work

Initial verification certifies that new work meets BS 7671, so there shouldn't be anything wrong with it. Any defects or omissions the inspector finds must be put right by the installer and re-inspected and retested before the EIC is issued. It isn't the inspector's job to fix them (GN3 2.1), and you don't "report" them as on a condition report: either it's right and certified, or it isn't finished.
