Safe isolation

Before you touch a conductor, you have to know it's dead and that it will stay dead. Safe isolation is the procedure that makes sure of both, and it's the first thing assessed in every practical.

Key takeaways

The Electricity at Work Regulations require work to be done dead unless live work is genuinely unavoidable. The procedure: identify the circuit and get permission; isolate it; secure the isolation with your own lock, keeping the key, and a warning label; prove your voltage indicator works on a known live source or proving unit; test that the circuit is dead between every pair of conductors (L–N, L–E and N–E on single phase); then prove the indicator again. Use a two-pole voltage indicator to HSE guidance GS38: finger guards, no more than 4 mm of exposed probe tip (2 mm or less preferred) and fused or current-limited leads. A non-contact "volt stick" or a multimeter isn't a suitable way to prove dead.

Why it matters

Most electricians who are killed at work die because they thought a circuit was dead when it wasn't: the wrong circuit was switched off, someone switched it back on, a borrowed circuit fed it from elsewhere, or the tester had failed. The EAWR require a way to cut off and isolate the supply (regulation 12), precautions to stop it being re-energised while you work (regulation 13), and live work only where it's unreasonable to make it dead, reasonable to work live, and suitable precautions are taken (regulation 14).

Fault-finding and some tests have to be done live, and those need their own risk assessment and precautions. Everything else is done dead.

The procedure

  1. Identify the circuit or equipment to be worked on, and the point of isolation. Get permission from whoever is responsible for the installation: switching off may affect other people, alarms or equipment.
  2. Isolate it with a suitable device: a double-pole isolator, the main switch, or the circuit's own circuit breaker or fuse. A functional switch, such as a light switch, isn't isolation.
  3. Secure the isolation: fit a lock-off device and your own padlock, and keep the key on you. Fit a warning notice. If you remove a fuse, keep it with you.
  4. Select an approved voltage indicator and check it, and its leads and probes, for damage.
  5. Prove the indicator works, on a known live source or a proving unit.
  6. Test the circuit at the point of work: line to neutral, line to earth and neutral to earth. On three-phase with neutral that's 10 tests: each line to each other line, each line to neutral, each line to earth, and neutral to earth.
  7. Re-prove the indicator on the same source. If it fails now, your "dead" reading means nothing.
  8. Only then start work.

Check for other sources too. Solar panels, batteries, generators and borrowed neutrals can all keep a circuit live after you've switched off the obvious supply. Amendment 4 of BS 7671 brings more of these into homes, such as battery storage, which now counts as a generating set.

    Test equipment and GS38

    The HSE guidance note GS38 sets out what test leads, probes and voltage indicators should be like:

    • Finger barriers on the probes, or a shape that stops your fingers sliding onto the metal.
    • No more than 4 mm of exposed metal at the probe tip. 2 mm or less, or spring-loaded retractable shrouds, are strongly recommended.
    • Fused leads (a high breaking capacity fuse, usually 500 mA or less) or current limiting, to limit the energy if the probes short across a live supply.
    • Well-insulated, flexible leads, long enough for the job but not so long they're clumsy, with no exposed conductors.
    • The right measurement category (CAT) rating for where it'll be used: higher categories for work nearer the origin of the supply.

    For proving dead, use a dedicated two-pole voltage indicator (to BS EN 61243-3) and its proving unit. A multimeter can be set to the wrong range, and a non-contact voltage detector only senses an electric field, so it can't prove a conductor is dead.
