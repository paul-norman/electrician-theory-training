Dead test bench

Practise the dead tests from the demonstrations on a virtual circuit: the three-step ring final test and the insulation resistance test, with faults you can switch on and a mystery mode to diagnose.

Key takeaways

Ring final, step 1: measure each conductor end to end (r1, rn, r2); line and neutral should match within 0.05 Ω, and the 1.5 mm² CPC of 2.5 mm² twin and earth reads 1.67 times higher. Step 2: cross-connect line and neutral of opposite legs and measure line to neutral at every socket: each reads about r1 + rn4. Step 3: the same with line and CPC; every socket reads about r1 + r24, and the highest is recorded as R1 + R2. Readings that rise towards the middle mean the legs are linked the wrong way; a socket that reads normally in step 2 but open in step 3 has line and neutral reversed. For insulation resistance, remove loads, test line to neutral, then line and neutral joined to earth.

How to use it

The ring below has six sockets and an optional spur. Choose a step, set the links at the board, then click or tap a socket to "plug in" and read the tester. Readings are calculated from the actual circuit, so a fault changes them the way it would on site. Fill the table, then compare with what you'd expect. In mystery mode, a hidden fault is put somewhere in the ring and you diagnose it from your readings.

What the readings tell you

  • Step 1 open circuit on any conductor: the ring is broken. Find and fix it before going on (on new work; on a periodic inspection you'd report it).
  • r1 and rn more than 0.05 Ω apart: usually a loose or poorly made connection, such as a screw clamping on insulation, in the higher conductor.
  • Step 2 or 3 readings low at both ends and highest in the middle: the legs are linked the wrong way at the board (same leg to same leg). Swap the links.
  • One socket open in step 3 but normal in step 2: line and neutral reversed at that socket. Step 2 can't see it, because it measures between the same two conductors whichever terminal they're on.
  • A slightly higher reading at one point: a spur, which is normal. Record the highest step 3 reading as R1 + R2.
  • Step 2 isn't recorded on the schedule of test results, but you still have to do it: it's what proves the ring and the line–neutral polarity at each socket.

Insulation resistance in practice

  • Check the tester first: leads apart should read off the scale, leads touching near zero. Then you know it's set up and the leads are in the right sockets.
  • Remove loads, such as lamps and plugged-in equipment, and isolate electronics (dimmers, timers, SPDs, smoke alarms). A lamp left in makes a line–neutral test read almost nothing, and the lamp even glows from the test voltage. A laptop could be damaged. Starting at 250 V is a sensible habit in case you've missed something.
  • Test line to neutral, then line and neutral joined to earth, at 500 V for a 230 V circuit. Close switches and operate two-way switches so every part is tested.
  • Once the voltage-sensitive equipment is reconnected, you can test line and neutral joined to earth at 250 V. Both conductors are at the same voltage, so nothing flows through the load.
  • Record the lowest reading (the worst); for continuity, record the highest. Minimum 1 MΩ by BS 7671, but a new circuit should read far higher. Investigate anything unexpectedly low.
  • Some testers let you "piggyback" one lead onto another so line and neutral are joined without disconnecting them. If yours doesn't, disconnect them and clip both under one lead.
